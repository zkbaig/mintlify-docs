## Topic Narrative
Week 4 tackles one of the most pivotal and challenging questions in entrepreneurial finance: How do we value a startup? Unlike mature companies, startups often have no profits (and sometimes minimal revenues), so traditional valuation techniques need modification. We introduce students to several methods that investors use, emphasizing practicality over theoretical perfection: Venture Capital Method: A popular approach for VC-stage companies. We explain the two-step process: (1) estimate the startup’s potential exit value in the future (e.g., in 5–7 years, if all goes well, what could it be acquired or IPO for?), and (2) discount that back to present using the high target return (often 30-50% annual for VC to account for risk). For example, if we think a startup could be worth \$100M in 5 years and a VC wants a 10x return, they might only invest at a $10M post-money valuation now . We’ll practice a simple calculation: say target exit \$50M in 5 years, target ROI 10x (which corresponds roughly to ~58% IRR over 5 years), then today’s post-money valuation would 10. 11. ◦ ◦ ◦ ◦ ◦ ◦

Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) for Startups: We mention that while in theory one could project cash flows and discount them, the projections for startups are extremely speculative. Traditional DCF often yields unrealistically wide ranges for startups. However, we discuss using scenario-based DCF (e.g., optimistic, base, pessimistic cash flow scenarios weighted by probability) or very high discount rates to reflect risk . In practice, pure DCF is rare for early-stage valuation but is used for later-stage or when negotiating in certain contexts. Comparables (Market Multiples): Investors also look at how similar companies are valued. For example, “Fintech apps are valued at 5× their annual recurring revenue in recent deals” or “Public SaaS companies trade at 10× revenue, so a high-growth private SaaS might aim for 5–8×.” We show students how to find or use multiples: either revenue multiple or user-based metrics (like \$/user valuations in social media, or \$/EBITDA for more stable businesses). If a startup has \$2M revenue and peers are at 5×, that suggests \$10M valuation. But we caution that using comparables requires adjusting for stage (a tiny startup will be at lower multiples due to growth and risk). The Role of Negotiation and Ownership Targets: We highlight that often valuation is determined not just by formulas but by negotiation and market dynamics . Founders want a high valuation to minimize dilution; investors want a reasonable valuation such that their stake can 10x in value. Early stage, sometimes it boils down to: how much money is needed and how much ownership the investor demands. For instance, many VCs historically want ~20–30% ownership in a round . If a startup needs \$2M, the post-money might be set around \$8M–\$10M so that \$2M buys ~25%. We discuss how cap tables work: pre-money valuation + investment = post-money valuation; the investor’s % = investment/post-money. Speaking of cap tables, we do a mini-primer: a cap table lists all the equity ownership: founders, investors, options, etc. We illustrate with a simple scenario: 2 founders own 60/40 initially. After a seed investment, the investor owns 20%, founders get diluted to 48% and 32%, and maybe a 10% option pool is set aside . We walk through how to do that math and stress the need to plan option pools for employees (which typically comes out of founder’s share if done pre-money). Students learn terms like pre-money valuation (value before new cash) and post-money (value after cash). We demonstrate on the board a cap table before and after an investment: e.g., Pre: Founder A 600k shares, Founder B 400k (total 1M shares = 100%). Then total shares 1.25M: investor 250k (20%), founder A 600k (~48%), founder B 400k (~32%). If we also need a 10% option pool post, we might create ~138k shares for options, diluting everyone accordingly – an example of how these moving parts interplay. We also mention convertible notes and SAFE notes as alternative financing instruments where valuation might be deferred, but with a cap. Just to expose them, e.g., a SAFE might say “convert at next round with a \$5M cap” – effectively setting an upper bound on valuation for that note conversion. Students should grasp that these are popular in seed stage to avoid valuation negotiation, but they do affect ownership later.

## Key Questions & Takeaways
What is the venture capital method of valuation and when is it used? **Answer:** The Venture Capital Method is a simple approach to early-stage valuation that focuses on the exit scenario . First, estimate the startup’s potential value at exit (say 5–7 years in the future). Then determine the investor’s required return multiple (for a high-risk startup, often 10× or more for that time frame). Finally, divide the future value by that target return to get the present post-money valuation. For example, if a startup could be worth \$50M in 5 years and the VC wants a 10× return, they’d invest at a \$5M post-money valuation now . This method is used when a startup has little in current financials to go on, so investors anchor on what it could be worth and work backward. It’s quick and straightforward, though obviously the estimates (exit value and required multiple) are rough. It often sets valuation in seed/Series A rounds in conjunction with ownership targets (e.g., the VC might also sanity-check that the valuation gives them ~20% ownership). The key takeaway is that VC valuations are driven by forward-looking potential and required return, not present earnings. Why might traditional Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) be less practical for valuing startups? **Answer:** DCF involves forecasting cash flows many years out and discounting them – it requires fairly predictable and stable cash flows to be meaningful. Startups, however, have highly uncertain and volatile prospects: their future cash flows are essentially guesses. A tiny change in assumptions (growth rate, margins a few years out) can swing the DCF valuation wildly. Also, the appropriate discount rate for startups is extremely high (to account for risk), which tends to diminish the present value of distant cash flows to near-zero, making the result not very informative. In essence, a DCF for a startup often either tells you “it’s worth almost nothing now” (if you use a high discount rate and modest projections) or, if you use optimistic projections, you’re basically just believing your guess. Investors usually prefer simpler heuristic methods (like VC method or comparables) and use DCF more for later-stage companies with revenues or for sanity checks. The takeaway: DCF is theoretically sound but impractical for early-stage valuations due to extreme uncertainty; other methods are favored in venture finance . How do investors use comparables (multiples) to value a startup? Give an example. **Answer:** Using comparables means looking at how similar companies are valued in the market – either in public markets or recent private funding rounds – and applying those multiples to the startup in 1. 15 2. 16 3. 34 question. For example, suppose software-as-a-service (SaaS) companies are commonly valued at ~10× their annual recurring revenue (ARR) in the public market. If our startup has \$2M ARR, a naive comparable valuation might be \$20M (2M × 10). Of course, we’d adjust for the startup’s size and growth (if it’s growing faster than public companies, maybe a higher multiple is justified; if it’s riskier, maybe lower). Another example: if a comparable early-stage startup with 1 million users sold for \ $50M, then our startup with 500k users might be notionally \$25M (assuming similar engagement and monetization). Investors find comparables by looking at ratios like Price-to-Revenue, Price-to- EBITDA (for profitable companies), or even per-user or per-subscriber valuations in certain industries. It grounds the valuation in market reality: ’What are others paying for companies like this?” However, no two startups are identical, so it’s more a reference point than a strict rule. The key is to pick relevant comparables (same sector, business model, similar stage if possible) and be aware of differences. For instance, in Canada valuations might historically have been a bit lower than Silicon Valley for similar metrics , something an investor might factor in. Overall, multiples give a quick, market-informed estimate to sanity-check a valuation. Explain the concepts of pre-money and post-money valuation, and how they relate to equity percentage for an investor. **Answer:** Pre-money valuation is the value of the company before the new investment comes in. Post- money valuation is the value after the investment is included. Numerically: post-money = pre- money + investment amount. The investor’s ownership percentage is determined by the fraction of the post-money that their investment represents. For example, if a startup is valued at \$8M pre- money and an investor puts in \$2M, the post-money valuation is \$10M. The investor’s \$2M is 20% of \$10M, so the investor will own 20% post-deal. The existing shareholders (founders, prior investors) who collectively had 100% pre-money will now own 80% (they got diluted but the pie grew). It’s crucial in negotiations: entrepreneurs often think in pre-money (“We believe we’re worth \ $X now”), and investors think in terms of how much of the company they want. Using the example above, if the founders wanted to raise \$2M at no less than \$8M pre (meaning they keep ≥ 80%), that aligns with an investor who’s okay with ~20%. If instead the investor wanted 33%, and \$2M was the fixed raise, they’d push for a \$4M pre (\$6M post, where 2/6 = 33%). So pre- and post-money are just different frames, but you must be clear which one you’re discussing. Cap tables and term sheets always specify these to avoid confusion (e.g., “\$2M investment at \$8M pre-money implies investor gets X%”). Students should understand that as more money is raised (with fixed pre-money), the investor percentage goes up (diluting others more), and vice versa. It’s a straightforward formula, but it’s the basis of how equity splits are determined in each round. What is an option pool and how does creating one affect founders’ ownership? **Answer:** An option pool is a block of shares set aside for future employees’ stock options. Investors often require an option pool be created (or topped up) at the time of investment so that there are enough shares to attract talent post-investment without immediately diluting the investor’s percentage. Importantly, option pools are typically carved out of the pre-money valuation – effectively diluting the founders. For example, an investor might say, “After our investment, 10% of the company should be reserved for an employee option pool.” If that pool wasn’t already in the cap table, the founders’ shares will be diluted to create it. In practice, if a startup is worth \$10M post-money and they need a 10% pool, about \$1M of that post-money value is assigned to the pool. So the effective pre-money for founders in that negotiation was \$9M, not \$10M, since the pool is like a “phantom co-founder” taking equity. Students should understand that while option pools don’t go to a single person, they represent future dilution mainly hitting founders. In negotiation, savvy founders try to size the pool 22 4. 5. 35 no larger than needed (so they don’t over-dilute) and also might push to count the pool as part of post-money (diluting everyone) rather than pre-money. But in early rounds, investors usually insist it’s created pre-money (from founder’s slice). The option pool is crucial for growth – startups need to grant stock options to hire key team members – so it’s a normal part of cap tables. Just remember: a new option pool is like issuing new shares, and if done pre-money, it’s essentially taken out of the founders’ portion. A seed investor bought 20% of a startup for \$500k. Later, a Series A investor puts in \$2M at a \$8M pre-money valuation. What is the post-money valuation, and how do the ownership percentages shift for all parties? **Answer:** Let’s break it down: Before Series A, we have seed investor at 20%, founders 80% (assuming only those parties). Pre-money \$8M means the company is considered worth \$8M before new money. The Series A adds \$2M, so post-money valuation = \$8M + \$2M = \$10M . Now, the Series A investor’s ownership = \$2M / \$10M = 20% of the company post-money. The remaining 80% is the pre-money shareholders (seed + founders combined). Previously, seed had 20% (founders 80%). These percentages now dilute proportionally within that 80%. Specifically: seed had 20% out of the 100%, which is 1/5 of the company pre, or 25% of the pre-money group (because founders were 80, seed 20, seed’s share of that pre-money group is 20/(20+80)=20%). After dilution, seed will have 20% of that remaining 80%. Another way: Seed’s new ownership = old ownership × (1 – new investor %). That is 20% × (1 – 0.20) = 20% × 0.8 = 16% post Series A. The founders collectively were 80% pre, now they get diluted to 80% × 0.8 = 64% post. The Series A takes 20%. We can check: 64 + 16 + 20 = 100%. Yes. So outcomes: post-money \$10M; Series A investor 20%, seed investor 16%, founders 64%. This illustrates how early investors and founders get diluted by new rounds. We can discuss that despite going from 80% to 64%, the founders’ shares value-wise may have increased (e.g., originally the whole company maybe after seed was valued at \$2.5M pre if \$500k was 20% -> meaning founders’ 80% was \$2M; now their 64% of \$10M is \$6.4M – they own less of a much bigger pie). That’s the principle of dilution: you own a smaller percentage but ideally of a more valuable company after the investment. It’s healthy as long as value grows sufficiently. This question forces students to practice cap table arithmetic and understanding dilution impacts.

It can be demoralizing too (employees see their equity value drop) and can give negative signal externally (less confidence in company). However, a down-round brings in needed cash to continue operations robustly, possibly preventing needing layoffs and allowing growth to resume. If the startup can afford to lose some staff without derailing progress, a moderate reduction might be better than a punitive financing. Communication and transparency will be key whatever path. In short, there's no easy answer: it's a choice between dilution (investor/ founder pain) and downsizing (employee pain). Use of AI Tools (Expanding on Academic Integrity regarding AI, as this is a hot topic.) We acknowledge AI tools like GPT-4 can be useful for idea generation and even improving writing. We don’t want to ban tools that are becoming part of business practice, but we need to ensure they’re used ethically and don’t undermine learning objectives. So: - Permitted uses: Brainstorming concepts, summarizing complex text you provide to it, getting examples or alternative phrasings, checking grammar. E.g., you might ask ChatGPT, “What are common risks a VC sees in early-stage startups?” to compare with your own list. This can spark ideas you might research further. Or use it to polish the language in your memo (after you’ve written a draft). - Prohibited uses: Having AI produce your assignment answers, exam responses, or any substantive content that you just copy and present as yours. For instance, you should not prompt ChatGPT with our assignment question and submit its essay as your own— that’s plagiarism. Also, do not use AI to circumvent calculations or analysis you’re expected to learn (e.g., don’t have it solve a cap table problem and give you the answer without you understanding it). - Disclosure requirement: If you do use AI in any part of an assignment, you must include an acknowledgment of how it was used. For example, at the end of your memo: “Note: Used ChatGPT to refine the clarity of the introduction” or “An AI tool was consulted for examples of market risks, which were then verified and incorporated by the author.” This transparency is required. Not only does it maintain honesty, it also lets us gauge how AI is aiding or possibly misleading you (teaching opportunity). - Accuracy caution: AI can produce incorrect or nonsensical answers that sound plausible (the “hallucination” problem). It might cite non-existent sources or get math wrong. You are responsible for verifying any factual or numerical output. If your assignment includes a “fact” or figure just because the AI said so and it’s wrong, that will be marked off. For example, if you say “According to PitchBook, 2022 VC funding was \$500 billion” because ChatGPT invented that stat, that’s on you (the real number was different ). Always cross-check with reliable sources. - Learning vs. Substitution: Our policy’s spirit is: use AI to enhance learning, not replace it. It’s similar to having a tutor or using Grammarly – tools that help you improve your work, which is fine. But the core thinking, analysis, and structure should be yours. Over- reliance on AI such that you couldn’t explain or replicate your work in an interview or without the tool defeats the purpose of the course. We will occasionally discuss in class how AI might solve a problem vs. how you solve it, as this is actually a relevant industry discussion too (e.g., in due diligence, some VCs use AI for market research – we can cover that).
