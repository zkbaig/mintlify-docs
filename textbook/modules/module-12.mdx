## Key Concepts

- **Revenue-based financing (RBF):** A financing structure in which investors receive a fixed percentage of revenue until a repayment cap is reached.
- **Venture debt:** Non-dilutive debt capital used to extend runway, typically alongside or after equity financing.
- **Rolling funds:** A continuous fundraising model for general partners (GPs) that accepts recurring commitments rather than a single vintage.
- **Continuation funds:** Investment vehicles that extend holding periods for select winners, often by purchasing stakes from earlier funds.
- **Tokenization:** Blockchain-based representations of equity or revenue shares that can enable fractional ownership and secondary trading.
- **Special purpose vehicles (SPVs):** Single-deal investment entities that pool investor capital for one specific opportunity.
- **Key performance indicator (KPI):** A quantitative metric used to track performance toward defined operating or strategic goals.
- **Initial public offering (IPO):** The formal process of listing a company’s shares on public markets for the first time.

## Main Content

### Chapter 1: Definitions and emerging models

This chapter establishes precise terminology for the emerging venture capital (VC) models that are reshaping financing pathways. It clarifies how each model operates so that comparisons later in the module are grounded in consistent definitions.

The future of venture capital (VC) includes **new funding models**, **data-driven investing**, and **diverse capital sources**—but each comes with specific use cases and failure modes.

- **Rolling funds:** Subscription-style capital commitments that allow recurring inflows instead of a single fund close, solving for faster fundraising when deal flow is continuous.
- **Revenue-based financing (RBF):** Repayment structures tied directly to revenue performance rather than fixed amortization schedules, solving for growth capital without heavy dilution.
- **Special purpose vehicles (SPVs):** Single-deal investment vehicles used to concentrate capital on one targeted opportunity, solving for targeted exposure without a full-fund commitment.

<Callout type="info" title="Innovation in structure">
Alternative models expand access but require new governance and transparency norms.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="What’s likely vs. speculative">
**Likely:** Rolling funds, SPVs, and RBF continue to grow as practical tools for speed, targeted exposure, and lower dilution.  
**Speculative:** Broad tokenized ownership and always-on secondary liquidity remain constrained by regulation, custody, and market depth.
</Callout>

#### Rolling funds: continuous capital formation

Rolling funds allow GPs to raise capital on a recurring basis, typically monthly or quarterly, instead of a single large close.  
**Problem solved:** Capital formation lag for managers with steady deal flow and time-sensitive opportunities.  
**Use case:** A manager can onboard new LPs quickly without waiting for a new fund cycle.  
**Fails when:** The GP needs stable long-duration capital, or administrative overhead from frequent closes reduces focus on investing.  
**Founder takeaway:** Expect faster access to capital but more frequent reporting requests.  
**Investor takeaway:** Prioritize managers with clear pipeline, consistent pacing, and disciplined reserve planning.

#### SPVs: deal-by-deal underwriting

SPVs pool investor capital for one specific investment, enabling targeted exposure without committing to a full fund.  
**Problem solved:** Targeted exposure to a single deal without a multi-year fund commitment.  
**Use case:** A founder wants a strategic investor syndicate for a single round, or an investor wants access to a specific late-stage deal.  
**Fails when:** The company needs follow-on support, reserves, or ongoing governance that a full fund typically provides.  
**Founder takeaway:** SPVs can bring in the right specialists, but you may need to line up future capital separately.  
**Investor takeaway:** Underwrite the single asset rigorously—there’s no portfolio to smooth outcomes.

#### RBF: non-dilutive growth capital

RBF provides capital that is repaid as a percentage of revenue until a cap is met, aligning repayment with performance.  
**Problem solved:** Growth capital for revenue-generating businesses that want to limit dilution.  
**Use case:** A SaaS company with predictable recurring revenue needs funding for growth without equity dilution.  
**Fails when:** Revenue is volatile, margins are thin, or growth requires large upfront spend that outpaces repayments.  
**Founder takeaway:** Model repayments carefully to avoid starving growth.  
**Investor takeaway:** Focus on cohort retention, gross margin, and revenue durability—not just top-line growth.

#### Tokenization: digital ownership and liquidity options

Tokenization uses blockchain tokens to represent equity, revenue share, or access rights, potentially enabling new liquidity paths.  
**Problem solved:** Fractional ownership and potential secondary liquidity for global or community-aligned capital.  
**Use case:** A global community-backed startup wants fractional ownership and secondary trading access within regulatory limits.  
**Fails when:** Regulatory clarity is missing, custody infrastructure is weak, or secondary markets lack liquidity.  
**Founder takeaway:** Treat tokenization as a compliance-heavy financing product, not a marketing feature.  
**Investor takeaway:** Underwrite legal, custody, and market-structure risks alongside business fundamentals.

### Chapter 2: Frameworks and decision tools

This chapter provides decision frameworks for matching company profiles to the most appropriate financing model. It emphasizes a comparative approach so that model selection is explicit, evidence-based, and repeatable.

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Trend map">
    - AI-assisted sourcing and diligence that accelerate screening and portfolio monitoring.
    - Angel collectives and syndicates that pool individual investors into coordinated rounds.
    - Secondary liquidity markets that allow earlier share transfers and partial exits.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Model selection">
    - Equity financing for high-growth trajectories that require patient capital.
    - RBF for steady cash flow businesses that can service revenue-linked repayments.
    - Crowdfunding for community alignment and stakeholder-driven brand momentum.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

#### Emerging model comparison matrix

| Model | Best fit | Capital source | Liquidity timing | Dilution impact |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rolling funds | Fast-moving deal flow and flexible fund sizing | Subscription commitments from limited partners (LPs) | Ongoing, evergreen fund cycles | Traditional equity dilution |
| RBF | Predictable revenue with measured growth | Revenue share repayments | Early, cash-flow based | Minimal equity dilution |
| Tokenization | Community-aligned or global investor access | Blockchain-based token holders | Secondary markets if allowed | Potential equity or revenue share |

#### Comparative framework: when each model wins/loses vs. traditional VC

| Model | Wins vs. traditional VC when... | Loses vs. traditional VC when... |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rolling funds | Speed and flexibility matter more than a fixed fund cycle. | The manager needs a stable, long-duration capital base with fewer administrative cycles. |
| SPVs | A single deal warrants targeted capital and curated investors. | The company needs ongoing support, reserves, and portfolio construction benefits. |
| RBF | Revenue is predictable and founders prioritize low dilution. | The company is pre-revenue or pursuing hypergrowth that needs patient equity. |
| Tokenization | Community ownership or global investor access is a strategic advantage. | Regulation, custody, or investor compliance makes traditional equity simpler. |
| Traditional VC | The company targets outsized growth and can accept dilution for expertise and follow-on capital. | The business has stable cash flow and low need for aggressive scale. |

#### Scenario-based exercise: choose a funding model

**Startup profile:**  
**NimbusOps** is a B2B SaaS company with \$80k MRR, 5% monthly churn, and a 30% month-over-month growth rate. The founder wants to avoid heavy dilution but needs capital to expand sales.  

**Task:** Choose the best funding model (traditional VC, rolling fund, SPV, RBF, or tokenization) and justify the choice in 3–5 sentences.

<details>
<summary><strong>Sample answer</strong></summary>

RBF is the best fit because NimbusOps has predictable recurring revenue and can repay capital as sales scale, minimizing dilution while funding growth. The main failure risk is cash-flow strain if growth slows or churn rises, so the founder should stress-test repayment scenarios. Traditional VC could be viable if the company needs large upfront capital and strategic support, but the founder’s dilution concerns point to RBF. Rolling funds or SPVs are less relevant because the company’s need is a flexible capital structure rather than a specific deal syndicate. Tokenization introduces regulatory and operational complexity without clear benefits for a B2B SaaS business.
</details>

**Interpretive summary:** Decision tools make model selection transparent by translating business fundamentals—cash flow, growth rate, and dilution tolerance—into an explicit funding recommendation.

### Chapter 3: Investor vs. founder perspective

### Chapter 6: Future trends and implications

This chapter synthesizes forward-looking signals to assess how venture capital may evolve over the next decade. It frames each trend in terms of founder and investor implications to support strategic planning.

**Drivers shaping VC’s next decade**

1. **AI-driven diligence and portfolio management**  
   - *Founder implication:* Expect deeper data requests and faster decision cycles.  
   - *Investor implication:* Differentiation shifts toward data infrastructure and underwriting speed.
2. **Liquidity innovation (secondaries, continuation funds, tokenized assets)**  
   - *Founder implication:* More options to de-risk ownership without a full exit.  
   - *Investor implication:* Portfolio construction includes liquidity planning, not just exits.
3. **Specialization by sector (climate, bio, defense, deep tech)**  
   - *Founder implication:* Sector-aligned investors can unlock non-capital advantages.  
   - *Investor implication:* Domain expertise becomes a core competitive edge.
4. **Capital decentralization (syndicates, rolling funds, global LPs)**  
   - *Founder implication:* More paths to capital, but heavier diligence complexity.  
   - *Investor implication:* GP branding and community-building grow in importance.
5. **Regulatory scrutiny and compliance expectations**  
   - *Founder implication:* Tokenization and global fundraising require legal readiness.  
   - *Investor implication:* Compliance and risk management costs rise.

**Interpretive summary:** The next decade is defined by faster information flows, more specialized capital, and new liquidity mechanisms, all of which raise the premium on disciplined governance and regulatory readiness.

## Pitfalls

<Callout type="warning" title="Pitfalls to Avoid">
- Treating new financing models as replacements rather than complements, which can lead to mismatched expectations.
- Ignoring regulatory constraints for tokens and crowdfunding, which can derail otherwise sound funding plans.
- Overconcentrating in hype sectors without diligence on unit economics, which undermines long-term portfolio resilience.
</Callout>
