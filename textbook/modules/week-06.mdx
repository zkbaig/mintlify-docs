## Topic Narrative
Term sheets are the blueprint of a VC deal – non-binding agreements outlining the key economic and control terms of an investment . While not legally binding (except certain provisions like confidentiality or no- shop clauses), they set the tone for the final agreements. A standard venture term sheet covers two broad areas: economic terms (valuation, amount raised, option pool, liquidation preferences, etc.) and control terms (board composition, voting rights, protective provisions). A crucial economic term is the liquidation preference , which dictates how proceeds are shared on exit. The most common is a 1× non-participating preference , meaning the investor can take back 1× their money or convert to common stock for their pro-rata share – whichever yields more . This is essentially downside protection. Some term sheets, especially in tougher funding climates, may demand a multiple liquidation preference (e.g. 2× or 3×), ensuring the investor gets double or triple their investment before the founders see any proceeds . Alternatively, a participating preference allows the investor to both get their preference amount and share in the remaining exit upside (a “double dip”), which is very investor- friendly . Term sheets might cap participation (e.g. participate until 2× return) to balance interests . Founders should negotiate for a 1× non-participating preference as the norm , since higher preferences significantly skew the payout in moderate exits. Another key element is anti-dilution protection , which shields investors in case the company raises a future round at a lower valuation (“down round”). The mild form is broad-based weighted average anti- dilution , which adjusts the preferred stock conversion price to reduce dilution to earlier investors proportionally. The harsh form is full ratchet , where the conversion price resets fully to the new low price (effectively giving earlier investors as many additional shares as needed to maintain their ownership) . Full ratchet heavily dilutes founders in a down round. Thus, founders prefer weighted average or, better yet, no anti-dilution beyond perhaps a narrow protection. Board composition and voting rights appear as control terms. A typical early-stage setup might be a 5- member board: 2 founders, 1 investor, and 2 independent seats (often chosen mutually). Term sheets will specify how many board seats investors get and sometimes that a specific individual from the VC firm will take one. This affects control: a 2-2-1 board (2 founders, 2 investors, 1 independent) could result in deadlock or investors aligning with the independent to outvote founders . Founders often try to maintain at least balanced or founder-leaning control pre-exit. Protective provisions are another control lever – these are veto rights investors negotiate on major decisions (e.g. selling the company, issuing new stock, changing the charter). Standard provisions might require investor director approval or a class vote of preferred shareholders for actions like raising new funding, changing the option pool, or making large expenditures. Such terms ensure investors can’t be diluted or marginalized without consent . However, too many vetoes can make the company unwieldy, so founders seek to keep them limited to big-ticket matters. Term sheet “mechanics” also involve things like vesting provisions for founders (investors may insist that founders’ stock vests over, say, 4 years with a 1-year cliff from the date of investment, to ensure they stick around) and drag-along rights (forcing minority shareholders to go along with a sale approved by a majority) . The term sheet may mention an option pool size to be created (which effectively lowers the pre-money valuation if it’s carved out pre-investment). It might also include a right of first refusal for 20 21 22 23 24 25 21 26 27 28 29 30 31 5 investors to buy shares that others are selling, pro rata rights to maintain ownership in future rounds, and sometimes registration rights for IPO planning. Importantly, market conditions influence term sheet terms. In frothy markets, term sheets become very “founder-friendly”: simple structures (1× non-participating pref, no onerous terms). In downturns, investor- friendly terms resurface. For example, after the market downturn in 2022, VCs began frequently adding multiple liquidation preferences, participation rights, and stronger anti-dilution to term sheets . One recent analysis noted that in early 2024, about 8% of new VC deals had >1× liquidation preference – the highest incidence in a decade . “Pay-to-play” provisions (forcing investors to invest pro rata in down rounds or lose some rights) also appear when investors want to encourage support in tough times . Understanding these shifting norms helps entrepreneurs know what’s negotiable. Ultimately, the term sheet stage is where the power balance between founders and investors is codified in terms – it’s critical to grasp how each clause affects economics and control before signing.
## Key Questions & Takeaways
What is a term sheet and is it binding? – It’s a non-binding agreement outlining the key terms of a VC investment . It covers valuation, amount to be invested, and investor rights. Generally, except for clauses like confidentiality or ’no-shop” exclusivity, the term sheet is not legally binding – it’s a blueprint that will be formalized in definitive contracts later. Why do VCs include liquidation preferences? – To ensure they recoup their investment before others if the company exits at a low price. A standard 1× non-participating liquidation preference means the VC gets back at least what they put in . If the exit is big, they’ll convert to common and take their percentage share. Preferences protect investors in downside scenarios, but higher multiples (2×, 3×) heavily favor investors and can leave founders with nothing unless the exit is substantial . What are “participating” preferred shares? – They are preferred shares that, on exit, get their preference and still participate in the remaining proceeds with common shareholders . For example, with a 1× participating preference, a VC first takes out their investment, then also gets, say, 20% of what’s left (if they own 20%). This double dip is very investor-friendly. Often, term sheets may cap participation (e.g. cap at 3× payout) to prevent unlimited upside to preferred. Founders usually aim for non- participating preferred to avoid this. How do anti-dilution provisions work? – They protect investors in a down round . The most common is weighted average anti-dilution : if new shares are issued at a lower price, the conversion price for the preferred is adjusted so investors get to convert into more shares than originally, lessening their dilution . In contrast, full ratchet anti-dilution resets the conversion price to the new low price, giving early investors the same price as the new investor – a much larger adjustment that can dramatically dilute founders. Founders prefer weighted average and may negotiate to exclude certain issuances (like small strategic grants) from anti-dilution. What control do investors gain via term sheets? – They usually gain a board seat or multiple seats , depending on ownership. They also get protective provisions (veto rights) on major decisions – for instance, issuing new stock, taking on debt, selling the company, changing the charter, etc., often requiring investor director or preferred majority approval . These ensure investors have a say in actions that could affect their interests. Additionally, investors may get information rights (regular financials), pro rata rights to maintain their stake in future rounds, and sometimes rights of first refusal on secondary sales by founders. How have term sheets trended recently? – They swing with the market. In founder-friendly times (e.g. 2021), term sheets were very simple: 1× non-participating prefs, no onerous terms, quick closes. In the recent downturn, investor-friendly terms have risen – e.g. multiple liquidation prefs, participating prefs, 32 33 33 34 1. 35 2. 21 23 3. 24 25 4. 36 5. 30 6. 6 and stricter anti-dilution appeared in more deals . Pay-to-play provisions became more common (forcing investors to invest in down rounds to avoid penalization) . Understanding current norms helps negotiators know where they have leverage. What is a “no-shop” clause? – It’s a term sheet provision that prevents the startup from soliciting other offers for a period of time (often 30–60 days) while the investor completes due diligence and legal docs. It is one of the binding parts of a term sheet. Once signed, the founders commit not to ’shop” the deal to other investors, giving the VC comfort that they have exclusivity during final negotiations.
## Required Readings
’Early-Stage Term Sheets” – Darden Note (2015). – An educational note that breaks down a standard venture term sheet clause by clause . It explains in plain language the meaning of terms like valuation and price per share, liquidation preference, anti-dilution, board rights, dividend provisions, etc., and their impact on founders. The note uses examples to show, for instance, how a 1× participating preference would work in a \$50M exit versus a non-participating pref. It also discusses typical ranges for terms and points out what is ’market” for early-stage deals. ’Understanding Venture Capital Term Sheets” – Silicon Valley Bank Startup Insights (2021). – A founder- oriented article outlining ten common term sheet terms and why they matter . It covers pre-money valuation, funding amount, liquidation preferences, conversion rights, anti-dilution, pay-to-play, board makeup, dividends, voting rights, and drag-along in a concise way. For each, it explains the basic premise (e.g. what a drag-along right is) and gives founder-focused negotiation tips (’aim for 1× non-participating pref”, ’push for broad-based anti-dilution”) . This reading provides a great checklist of terms to understand when reviewing a term sheet. Excerpt from ’Venture Deals” by Brad Feld & Jason Mendelson (4th ed., 2019, Chapter on Terms). – This book chapter, written by veteran VCs, demystifies term sheet provisions with a touch of humor. It delves into not just what each term means but why* it exists. For example, it discusses vesting for founders’ stock (to ensure founders don’t bail early) and protective provisions* from the VC’s perspective. Feld and Mendelson share anecdotes of term negotiations, like how an overzealous anti- dilution clause nearly killed a company in a down round. The authors also provide sample language from actual term sheets. Reading this gives students a nuanced understanding of how VCs and lawyers think when drafting terms and what founders should watch out for.

## In-Class Activity
Term Sheet Negotiation Role-Play Activity Name: “The Term Sheet Tug-of-War” Objectives: To simulate a negotiation between founders and investors on key term sheet terms, building students’ understanding of trade-offs and priorities in deal-making. Setup: The class is divided into two sides – Team Founder and Team VC . Within those, small groups of 2–3 students receive a mock term sheet for “DataSecure Inc,” a fictional Series A company. The term sheet intentionally includes several negotiable points: a 1× participating liquidation preference, a full ratchet anti- dilution clause, a requirement for a 4-member board (2 investors, 1 founder, 1 independent), a 20% option pool pre-money, and a valuation that both sides think could move (founders think it’s low, VCs think it’s high). Instructions: Each side (founders vs. VCs) gets 20 minutes to internally strategize. Founders list which terms they most want to change (e.g. convert participating pref to non-participating, remove full ratchet, get a board seat back, or raise valuation). VCs list what they are willing to concede and what they consider deal-breakers (e.g. perhaps they’ll drop participation if valuation stays put, but insist on at least weighted average anti-dilution). Then a live negotiation ensues: one small group from each side comes to the front and negotiates term by term, with the instructor moderating. For example, founders might open with “We’d like to eliminate the participating feature of the liquidation preference,” and VCs might counter with a justification or a conditional trade (“We can do that if the valuation lowers or if we get two board seats”). Classmates can chime in as “advisors” whispering suggestions. Resolution: After 15–20 minutes of negotiation, the class sees what a possible compromise looks like – say, the parties agree to 1× non-participating pref (founders win), broad-based weighted average anti- dilution instead of full ratchet (founders win), the board becomes 5 seats (2 founders, 2 VCs, 1 independent – balanced), and the pre-money valuation is adjusted slightly upward as a concession to founders. The option pool might remain as is if not contested. The instructor then debriefs: highlighting how each change affects outcomes (e.g. in a middling exit, switching off participation can mean millions more to common shareholders ). Students reflect on the give-and-take nature of term sheets – everything is connected . This active exercise reinforces comprehension of each term’s importance and how savvy negotiation can protect the entrepreneur’s upside while still meeting investors’ downside protection needs.
## Case Study
Control or Growth? – The NewBoard Corp Term Sheet Background: NewBoard Corp is a B2B SaaS startup that just received a term sheet for a \$8M Series A. The lead is a well-known VC firm. The case opens with founders celebrating the valuation, which is slightly higher than expected at a \$40M pre-money . However, as they read through the term sheet, they grow concerned about control implications. Key details: - Valuation & Investment: \$40M pre-money, \$8M investment (so post-money \$48M; VC gets ~16.7% ownership). The founders are pleased with this high valuation. - Liquidation Preference: 1× participating preferred with no cap. (Founders are unsure what this fully implies but sense it’s not ideal.) - Board Structure: 5 seats proposed – 2 designated by investors (including one independent mutually agreed but essentially chosen by the lead investor), 2 by founders, 1 independent agreed mutually. In effect, the lead investor would control 2 seats plus likely influence the “independent” – potentially 3 votes. - Protective Provisions: Any sale of the company, new financing, or change to bylaws requires approval of a majority of preferred (i.e. the new investor). - Founder Vesting: The two founders must each “re-vest” 50% of their shares over 3 years, with a 1-year cliff from closing. - Other 24 8 terms: standard anti-dilution (broad-based weighted), pro rata rights for investor, 10% option pool to be added (pre-money). Dilemma: The founders face a classic “price vs. control” decision. The valuation is great (they retain more equity on paper), but the terms give the investor significant control. They worry: with this structure, could they be replaced as CEO or forced into decisions? One founder remarks that effectively the investor could outvote them on the board in some scenarios and has vetoes on major items. The case narrative describes their internal debate and a tense call with the VC where the VC explains these terms are “standard” given the high valuation. The founders consider pushing back on participation and board makeup, but fear the deal falling through. Questions for Analysis: - Q1: Evaluate the impact of the participating liquidation preference in this deal. How would it affect the founders’ proceeds in an exit, say at \$50M versus \$200M? (Students calculate that at a \$50M sale, the VC with 1× participating would take \$8M off the top plus 16.7% of the remaining \$42M, totaling about \$15M, whereas if non-participating, the VC would choose either \$8M or \$8M as common share value – in both cases \$8M – leaving much more for founders. At \$200M, participation matters less as the pro-rata exceeds the preference. This illustrates how participation mainly hurts in moderate exits .) - Q2: Does the board structure truly give the VC control? – (With 2 investor seats and 1 “independent” that the investor may effectively pick, the investor bloc could be 3 vs. 2 founders. That means on many matters, yes, the VC could have effective control. Students should recognize the risk: the founders could be outvoted on hiring a new CEO, raising a down-round, etc. This board setup is more investor-leaning than founder- friendly. Perhaps they’d negotiate for 2-1-2 (Founders-Investors-Independents) to avoid any one faction dominating .) - Q3: How do the protective provisions and re-vesting requirement affect the founders? – (Protective provisions give the investor a veto on selling the company – the founders can’t accept an offer without investor approval, which could be problematic if, say, the investor wants a higher price. Re-vesting means if a founder leaves before 3 years, they could lose a chunk of their shares, aligning founder incentives with the investor’s timeline. Students might say re-vesting is common in Series A, but perhaps they negotiate that only if a founder leaves voluntarily or is fired for cause will the company repurchase unvested shares, to protect against arbitrary removal.) - Q4: If you were the founders’ advisor, which terms would you push back on most strongly, and how? – (Likely the participating preference and board control. Students may suggest accepting the high valuation but asking for non-participating pref (or at least a cap on participation). They could also request the independent board member be truly independent (mutually agreed and not an affiliate of the VC). They might live with vesting as it’s not uncommon, and anti-dilution is already weighted average which is reasonable. This prioritization exercise teaches which terms “matter” most in long run.) - Q5: Should the founders accept this deal if the VC refuses to budge? Why or why not? – (This invites discussion: Some may argue that control terms are too onerous – the founders risk losing strategic control and some economic upside, which could backfire. Others may note that the firm is prestigious and the money is valuable for growth – maybe accept but be very aware going forward. It’s a judgment call: if the startup really needs the \$8M and alternatives are scarce, they might take it but try to perform well to avoid conflicts. If they have other investor interest, they might walk away to find better terms. The class can debate the trade-offs.) Analysis: This case immerses students in reading a term sheet and understanding the interplay between valuation and terms . A high valuation can come with strings attached. By analyzing NewBoard’s situation, 24 28 9 students learn that not all money is equal – the cheapest equity (high valuation) might cost you control. The case discussion should draw out that an “industry standard” term in one era (e.g. participating prefs became more common in downturns ) might still be negotiable if you have leverage. The instructor can share outcomes from similar real cases – for instance, founders who accepted aggressive terms and later regretted it when those terms came into play, versus founders who successfully negotiated them away. The key takeaway is a nuanced understanding of term sheet mechanics and that entrepreneurs must balance economic upside with governance rights when structuring a deal.
