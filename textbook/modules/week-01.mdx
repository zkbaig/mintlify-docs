## Topic Narrative
Entrepreneurial finance is the domain of financial decision-making for new ventures, where traditional corporate finance rules often bend to fit the extreme uncertainty and high-growth potential of startups. This introductory module maps out the landscape of players and capital sources that fuel entrepreneurship. Unlike established companies with steady revenues and collateral, startups typically rely

The vast majority of new businesses begin with “bootstrapping” – tapping personal funds or informal investments – while only a tiny fraction ever secure formal venture capital backing . (For context, around 77% of entrepreneurs use personal savings to launch their business, whereas just ~0.05% of startups are funded by VC investors .) We examine why external financing is both difficult to obtain and crucial for scaling promising ventures. Startups face a “financing gap” : they are too risky for banks (few assets, unpredictable cash flows) yet often need more capital to grow than founders can provide. This is where venture capital and angel investors step in – in exchange for equity ownership, they provide high-risk capital and mentorship to help startups grow. Students will learn about the funding lifecycle of a startup – from pre-seed and seed funding through Series A/B growth rounds, and eventually exit events like IPOs or acquisitions. We also discuss how North America’s entrepreneurial finance ecosystem has evolved. For example, the U.S. boasts deep pools of venture capital concentrated in tech hubs like Silicon Valley, while Canada’s ecosystem – though smaller – features significant government involvement alongside a growing private VC community. Canadian entrepreneurs often supplement private financing with non-dilutive support such as R&D grants (e.g. IRAP) and public investment funds (e.g. BDC Capital), reflecting a model where government plays a larger role in early-stage funding. Throughout the course, we will highlight both tech and non-tech startups to demonstrate that while venture capital has been synonymous with high-growth technology ventures, sectors like life sciences, clean energy, consumer products, and social enterprises each navigate unique financing paths. By the end of this week, students should grasp the big-picture structure of entrepreneurial finance – who the key players are, how deals flow, and why financing early-stage ventures requires a fundamentally different approach from financing mature companies.
## Key Questions & Takeaways
What makes financing a startup different from financing an established company? **Answer:** Startups have extreme uncertainty, little operating history, and few tangible assets, so they can’t easily access traditional bank loans or public markets. Financing a startup usually involves equity investments from angels or VCs who accept higher risk for potential high growth, whereas established companies rely more on predictable cash flows and debt financing. Venture investors also often provide mentorship and networks, not just capital, to help navigate the high risk . What are the primary sources of funding for new ventures? **Answer:** Early on, most founders rely on personal savings and contributions from friends and family. As the venture grows, they may seek angel investors (affluent individuals investing their own money) or join accelerators . For larger scale, startups turn to venture capital firms that invest pooled funds for equity. Additional sources include strategic corporate partners (industry companies investing for strategic reasons), bank or government loans (for those ventures that qualify), and crowdfunding platforms that allow the public to fund the project in exchange for pre-orders or equity. Each source has different costs and expectations attached. How common is venture capital funding among startups? **Answer:** Extremely rare – only a very small percentage of startups ever raise venture capital. Roughly 0.05% of startups are funded by VC firms . Most new businesses rely on personal funds or smaller-scale financing rather than formal VC investment. Venture capital is thus reserved for 3 4 5 1. 6 2. 3. 5 3 exceptional cases: startups targeting very large markets and seeking rapid, exponential growth. The takeaway is that while VC gets disproportionate attention in media, the vast majority of entrepreneurs will fund their businesses through other means. What is the typical funding lifecycle of a high-growth startup? **Answer:** A high-growth startup often progresses through stages of increasing investment: it might start with pre-seed/seed funding (from founders, angels, or seed funds) to build a prototype and get initial traction. Next is a Series A round (usually the first significant VC-led investment) to scale up product development and marketing. If growth continues, Series B, C, etc. follow, bringing in larger checks to expand nationally or internationally. Eventually, if successful, the startup reaches an exit – such as an acquisition by a larger company or an IPO – allowing investors to realize returns. At each stage, investors conduct due diligence and base funding on hitting milestones (customers, revenue, etc.), and earlier-round investors may participate or new ones join as the company’s valuation rises. How does Canada’s startup financing landscape compare to the U.S.? **Answer:** Canada’s venture ecosystem is smaller in scale – Canadian startups raised roughly CAD $7.9B in VC across 592 deals in 2024 , whereas the U.S. sees many times that amount annually. Fewer Canadian startups reach “unicorn” status, and large late-stage deals are less common. However, Canada benefits from strong government support for early-stage companies (through grants, public funds, and tax credits), which can fill some funding gaps. U.S. startups typically have access to a greater number of VC firms and larger pools of private capital, often allowing for bigger funding rounds. Both countries follow similar funding stages, but Canadian founders may rely relatively more on non-dilutive funding (grants, loans) or have to court U.S. investors for growth rounds. Why might a founder choose not to seek venture capital? **Answer:** Not every business fits the VC model that demands rapid scaling and an exit. Founders may avoid VC funding to retain ownership and control , to grow at a deliberate pace, or to maintain the company’s mission without outside pressure. VC investment comes with expectations of aggressive growth and eventual sale of the company; founders who prefer to run a stable, profitable business (often called a “lifestyle business”) might opt for alternatives like bank loans, crowdfunding, or bootstrapping. In some cases, the business can thrive with customer revenue alone, avoiding the dilution and oversight that come with VC money. Ultimately, seeking VC is a trade-off: it can accelerate growth dramatically, but it also means ceding some control and aligning with investors’ timeline for returns.

## In-Class Activity
Funding Pathways Brainstorm Setup: The class is divided into small groups of 3–4 students. Each group receives a one-page scenario describing a different hypothetical startup at an early stage. For example: (a) a software app with potential for viral growth; (b) a hardware device requiring manufacturing; (c) a biotech research project; (d) a local service business expanding regionally. Each scenario provides basic info (industry, product, early traction, capital needs). Materials: Printed or digital scenario handouts for each group; flip charts or whiteboard space for groups to outline their funding plan. Instructions (Step-by-Step): Identify Funding Needs: Groups begin by discussing their scenario startup’s goals and estimating how much capital it realistically needs to reach the next major milestone (e.g. launch product, achieve certain revenue). They list expected expenses or investments (development, hiring, marketing, etc.). Brainstorm Sources: For each funding need, groups brainstorm possible sources. They consider all options introduced (personal/FFF money, angels, VC, bank loans, crowdfunding, grants). Which sources make sense given the startup’s industry and scale? For example, the biotech venture might consider government R&D grants, whereas the software app might aim for a tech accelerator or angel round. Choose a Funding Path: Each group decides on a recommended funding strategy for their startup for the next 1–2 years. This could be a single source or a combination (e.g. bootstrap initially, then seek an angel; or apply to an accelerator then raise a seed VC round). They outline the timing and rationale: ’We will do X now to raise \$Y, which should last Z months, then do….” Prepare to Present: Groups summarize their plan on the flip chart: listing chosen funding source(s), the amount to raise, and 2–3 reasons why this approach is suitable for the scenario. (~15 minutes for steps 1–4). Group Presentations: Each group presents their scenario and funding plan in a 3-minute informal pitch to the class. They explain why they picked that funding route and how it meets the startup’s needs. Facilitation: The instructor prompts deeper thinking with questions: “Why would a VC be (un)interested in this business? What are the downsides of taking that grant or loan? What happens if you can’t raise the next round?” During presentations, the instructor notes the diversity of approaches on the board. Expected Outputs: After all presentations, the class discusses differences. Key takeaways are recorded, such as: Not all startups need or attract VC – many thrive on alternative financing. Students should articulate why a certain source is appropriate (e.g. ’Our hardware startup chose crowdfunding to validate product demand and raise pre-order revenue, avoiding dilution at an early stage.” ). ## Case Study
GreenRidge Brewing Co. – A Funding Dilemma Background: Nate Williams and Sarah Li founded GreenRidge Brewing Co. in Ontario four years ago out of a mutual passion for craft beer. Nate, an engineer-turned-brewer, developed a lineup of unique brews using locally sourced ingredients. Sarah, with a marketing background, built a loyal community of beer enthusiasts through social media and taproom events. Starting in 2021 with a $150,000 loan from family and $100,000 of personal savings , the duo leased a modest brewery space with an attached taproom. By 2023, GreenRidge had a strong local reputation for quality craft beer and a friendly taproom vibe. The brewery was operating near full capacity on its 10-barrel brewing system, selling out most of its weekly production. Annual revenue in 2023 reached CAD $1.2 million, with a modest $100,000 profit (Exhibit 1) . Encouraged by their success, Nate and Sarah began contemplating how to expand. The Expansion Opportunity: Local demand suggested GreenRidge could easily double sales if it had more brewing capacity and space. Sarah outlined two growth paths: Slow, Organic Growth: Reinvest GreenRidge’s profits and perhaps take a small bank loan to expand gradually. For example, purchase a few additional fermenting tanks and hire two more brewers, which could increase output ~50% over two years. They could likely open one additional taproom location in a neighboring city within three years using accumulated profits and a local business loan. This plan is relatively low-risk and lets them maintain 100% ownership, but growth would be incremental and slow. Rapid Expansion with Outside Investment: Raise a significant sum (around $2 million ) from external investors to aggressively scale. With \$2M, they could immediately purchase a larger brewing system (50-barrel capacity), open two new taproom locations in high-traffic urban neighborhoods, and invest in a canning line to distribute GreenRidge beer to retail stores. This aggressive plan might triple production within two years. Sarah projected that under this scenario, revenue could reach ~$5 million by 2025 and possibly $10+ million by 2028 if all went well (Exhibit 2). However, pursuing this path meant giving up a share of ownership and having outside stakeholders who would expect substantial growth and eventual returns. Nate was initially cautious about the rapid expansion idea—he worried about overextending and losing the community-focused vibe that made GreenRidge special. But Sarah argued the craft beer market was getting competitive; if they didn’t seize the opportunity to grow, a rival might, or a well-funded new entrant could quickly eclipse them. Indeed, Toronto had seen a boom in craft breweries, and a few had attracted investor funding to scale up. GreenRidge risked being left behind if it stayed small. Considering Venture Capital: Neither founder had experience with institutional investment, so they researched funding options and sought advice. They learned that venture capital investors typically look for businesses that can scale nationally or globally and potentially return many times the investment. A brewery is a brick-and-mortar, capital-intensive business – not a typical “hockey stick growth” tech startup – so the fit with VC was unclear. On the other hand, a few craft beverage brands had grown big and been acquired by global beverage companies. (For example, a well-known Canadian craft brewery was bought by a multinational for ~$50 million, rewarding its early investors.) Sarah wondered: Could

Through a startup networking event, Nate and Sarah met Priya Kapoor, an associate at Maple Leaf Ventures , a VC firm interested in local consumer brands. Priya agreed to evaluate GreenRidge. She was intrigued by GreenRidge’s strong customer loyalty and revenue growth (50% increase from 2022 to 2023, as Exhibit 1 shows). However, as a venture investor, Priya had to consider whether the brewery could realistically achieve the kind of scale and exit value that a VC fund needs. Maple Leaf Ventures typically aimed for each investment to have a path to at least a $50–100 million exit within 5–7 years. Financial Projections: Nate and Sarah prepared forecasts to present to Priya. Under the organic growth plan , they projected reaching about $4 million in annual revenue five years from now (with perhaps $0.8 million in pre-tax profit) by slowly adding capacity and one new location. Under the VC-funded expansion plan (with a $2M injection now), they forecasted hitting $10 million in revenue in five years, with an estimated $2 million profit, assuming successful scaling to multiple locations and retail distribution (Exhibit 2). Achieving the aggressive plan would require opening 3–4 new brewpubs and securing shelf space in major retail beer outlets across Ontario. These projections were ambitious, and Priya knew to treat them with caution – nearly every startup’s projections paint a rosy scenario. Ownership and Control Considerations: At present, Nate and Sarah owned 100% of GreenRidge (aside from repaying the family loan). If they brought in a VC, that investor would take a significant equity stake. For instance, Priya discussed a hypothetical: Maple Leaf Ventures could invest $2M at, say, a $6M pre- money valuation (implying an $8M post-money valuation). That would give the VC 25% ownership post- investment. The founders’ combined stake would drop to 75% (Nate and Sarah split that between them), and the VC would likely demand a board seat and customary term sheet provisions (like liquidation preferences to ensure they get paid back first in an exit). Future funding rounds would dilute them further. Nate and Sarah understood this trade-off intellectually, but it was hard to imagine not being the sole owners of their “baby.” Priya had to consider Maple Leaf’s perspective: For a 25% stake to be worthwhile, GreenRidge’s exit value in a few years would need to be much higher. For example, if Maple Leaf wanted at least a 5–10x return on that $2M, they’d be looking for ~$20M–$40M just for their share , meaning an exit north of $80–$160 million company-wide. That would likely require expanding well beyond Ontario and achieving a dominant regional brand – a tall order in the fragmented craft beer industry. Priya was intrigued by GreenRidge but recognized it might be a better candidate for a smaller-scale investor (like an angel or growth equity for established breweries) rather than a VC expecting Silicon Valley-like returns. Alternatives to VC: Meanwhile, Nate and Sarah had other funding options to weigh. A local angel investor – a successful restaurateur who loved their beer – had expressed interest in investing $500,000 for a minority stake. That amount could fund one new taproom and some equipment upgrades, a middle ground approach. Also, a regional economic development agency offered low-interest loan programs for small manufacturers; GreenRidge could likely secure around $250,000 in a government-backed loan given their profitable track record. Combining an angel investment and a loan (total $750k) might allow them to open 1–2 new locations over a few years without the pressure-cooker expectations of VC. The founders discussed these scenarios: taking a smaller infusion and growing at a moderate pace versus the big-bet venture route. 7 By the end of their meeting, Priya did not give an answer but requested more information: she asked Nate and Sarah to research the total addressable market for craft beer in Canada and to think about an exit strategy (who might acquire them if things go well?). She would bring GreenRidge to Maple Leaf’s partners meeting for discussion. Nate and Sarah drove home pondering their decision. On one hand, teaming with a VC and $2M could turn GreenRidge into a powerhouse brand – Sarah found that prospect exciting. On the other hand, Nate felt that bringing in investors who might eventually push to sell the company conflicted with his vision of running a community-centric business for the long term. They also realized taking VC money now wasn’t a guarantee of success – it might just as easily end with a failed expansion and loss of control of their brewery. The founders sat down to weigh their priorities: growth vs. control, speed vs. sustainability. The entrepreneurial finance ecosystem was offering them multiple paths – now they had to choose the path that aligned best with their ambitions and GreenRidge’s true potential .
