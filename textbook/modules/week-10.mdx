## Topic Narrative
Venture capital funds are typically organized as limited partnerships , creating a distinct relationship between Limited Partners (LPs) – who contribute the capital – and General Partners (GPs) – who manage the fund and make investment decisions. Understanding this LP–GP partnership is crucial, as it underpins the incentives, governance, and ultimate success of venture investing. A standard VC fund has a fixed lifespan (often ~10 years with extensions) during which GPs deploy capital into startups, manage those investments, and seek exits to return capital (and profits) to LPs. The economic arrangement usually follows the “2 and 20” model : GPs receive an annual management fee of about 2% of the fund’s committed capital to cover operations, and carried interest equal to 20% of the fund’s profits (after returning capital to 28 5 LPs) as their upside reward . This structure aligns GPs’ incentives with performance – they only earn carry if the fund’s investments gain value – while providing LPs with professional management of high-risk, illiquid startup portfolios. LPs in venture funds include institutional investors like pension funds, university endowments, foundations, corporate strategic investors, family offices, and high-net-worth individuals. These LPs commit capital for the long term and entrust GPs to make prudent investments. A key element of the relationship is alignment of interest : LPs want GPs to act in the LPs’ best financial interests, so fund terms are negotiated to align incentives. For example, GPs usually invest their own money into the fund (often ~1–2% of fund size) as a GP commitment , ensuring they have “skin in the game.” Many funds also have a preferred return (hurdle rate) around 7–8% that the fund must achieve for LPs before GPs can collect carry . Such terms motivate GPs to generate strong returns. At the same time, LPs agree to the carried interest model to handsomely reward GPs who do succeed – this upside potential helps attract top investment talent to the venture industry. The Limited Partnership Agreement (LPA) codifies these economics and other governance provisions, like how decisions are made, what activities GPs can/can’t do, and reporting requirements to LPs. The LP–GP relationship goes beyond contracts; it’s fundamentally built on trust, communication, and alignment of vision . LPs perform careful due diligence when choosing VC funds, evaluating the GPs’ track record, strategy, team expertise, and value-add to startups. Once committed, an LP typically cannot withdraw capital (commitments are drawn down over time via capital calls), so they rely on periodic reports and advisory meetings for transparency. Successful GPs often treat LPs as partners, providing regular updates, candid assessments of portfolio health, and early communication of challenges or needs . For instance, if exits are delayed (as they have been in recent years), GPs might proactively discuss extension of fund life or secondary sale opportunities with LPs. Conversely, a breakdown in communication or misaligned expectations can strain the partnership. A notable example is during the dot-com bust and again in the 2022 downturn – many funds had to mark down portfolio values and extend timelines, testing LPs’ patience. The best GPs navigated this by being transparent and prioritizing some liquidity (distributions) to LPs whenever possible . Venture fund structure also encompasses fund size, strategy, and lifecycle , all of which affect LP–GP dynamics. Larger funds (e.g., mega-funds of $1B+ raised by top-tier firms) may have more institutional LPs and deploy across later-stage deals, whereas smaller micro-VC funds (say $50M) often involve niche strategies and closer-knit LP bases (like individuals or small family offices). In recent times, a “flight to quality” has been evident: many LPs have concentrated commitments into established VC firms, making it hard for emerging managers to raise capital . For example, in 2024, the top 10 VC firms accounted for nearly half of all VC fundraising , as LPs doubled down on names with proven track records during uncertain markets. This dynamic puts pressure on new GPs, even those with strong backgrounds, to convincingly answer LPs’ tough questions and differentiate their strategy. The relationship can thus be uneven – when VC markets are hot and returns strong, LPs eagerly commit (and often compete for allocation in elite funds), but in lean times, GPs must court LPs and prove their worth through clear strategy and evidence of value creation . Another aspect is fund governance and LP rights . LPs typically have an Advisory Committee (LPAC) composed of representatives from several major LPs, which advises on conflicts of interest, approves certain transactions (like GP handling of valuation or extensions), and generally provides oversight. However, LPs do not micromanage individual investments – those decisions are the GPs’ domain. Instead, 29 30 30 31 32 28 33 34 35 36 37 6 LPs ensure alignment by setting terms and monitoring results. If a GP violates terms or consistently underperforms, LPs have remedies like termination clauses or removal for cause , though these are rarely invoked due to relationship damage. More commonly, dissatisfied LPs simply decline to invest in the GP’s next fund, which is a powerful signal. In recent years, novel fund structures and practices have emerged, influencing LP–GP relations. Examples include rolling funds (continuous fundraising models), opportunity funds (side funds for later-stage follow-ons), and GP-led secondary transactions (where GPs sell or restructure portfolio assets into new vehicles to extend holdings). These innovations can be beneficial – e.g., continuation funds can give LPs the choice to cash out or roll over into a new vehicle if a fund’s marquee company needs more time to exit. But they also require extra communication and trust , since LPs must rely on GPs’ judgment that such moves are in their best interest (regulators and LP associations like ILPA have issued guidance to ensure fairness in these scenarios). Overall, the LP–GP partnership in venture capital is a delicate balance of finance and relationship : GPs need to deliver returns and stewardship; LPs need to provide stable capital and support. When done right, it’s symbiotic – as one VC adage goes, “happy LPs, happy life,” meaning that tending to LP interests and transparency ultimately enables GPs to raise future funds and continue investing in innovation.
## Key Questions & Takeaways
How do VC fund economics align incentives? The classic 2% management fee and 20% carry structure motivates GPs to maximize fund performance while covering their costs . The management fee ensures GPs can focus full-time on investments (covering salaries, due diligence, etc.), but is typically just enough to operate (and often tapers down after the investment period). Real profit for GPs comes from carried interest – they earn a share of upside only after LPs receive back their capital (and usually a preferred return) . This means LPs and GPs both win when startups exit successfully. If a fund doesn’t perform, carry might be zero, aligning risk-sharing. Some funds incorporate hurdles (e.g., 8% return) before carry kicks in, further aligning with LPs’ expectations of minimum returns. What is the role of LPs and how do they evaluate GPs? LPs are the capital providers and usually passive partners, but they are sophisticated and conduct due diligence much like VCs vet startups. They assess a GP team’s track record (IRRs, DPI) , strategy focus, deal sourcing network, and team stability. Many LPs – especially institutional ones – prefer GPs with consistent top-quartile returns or unique access to deals. In fundraises, GPs must often answer tough questions from LPs about how they’ll add value, manage risk, and differentiate in the market . Building trust is crucial: LPs need confidence that GPs will be transparent and prudent stewards of their capital over a decade- long fund life. A GP’s reputation among existing LPs is key – negative references or misaligned behaviors (like taking on too much personal leverage or conflicts of interest) can deter new LPs. How do communication and reporting strengthen LP–GP relations? Regular, honest communication is the cornerstone of LP confidence . GPs typically provide quarterly reports detailing portfolio company updates, fund financials, and any material events. Many also hold annual general meetings (AGMs) where LPs can interact with the GP team and sometimes portfolio CEOs. In challenging times (e.g., when exits are delayed or a company is faltering), GPs who proactively inform LPs and share their plan to address the issue tend to maintain trust. Clear communication also means no surprises: LPs should not be blindsided by, say, a sudden write-off of a major investment or a request to extend the fund’s term. What mechanisms protect LP interests? LP agreements include covenants and clauses to ensure GPs act prudently. Common protections: key person clauses (if key partners leave, fund activity pauses unless LPs vote to continue), restricted investments (funds usually can’t invest outside agreed scope or take on excessive debt, etc., without consent), and GP commitment (requiring GPs to invest their own capital into the fund, aligning interests). An LP Advisory Committee (LPAC) often monitors conflict of interest situations (e.g., if the fund wants to invest in a GP’s relative’s startup, or do a follow-on in a company where some LP has a stake). Additionally, LPs typically get most- favored-nation (MFN) clauses on terms, and audit rights to inspect fund records. These mechanisms, while rarely used punitively, serve as guardrails to encourage good governance by GPs and protect LPs from mismanagement or unfair treatment. How do external conditions affect LP–GP dynamics? The broader market directly impacts this relationship. For instance, in recent years venture funds experienced lower distributions due to the IPO slowdown . This has made LPs more scrutinizing and sometimes impatient for liquidity – a metric called DPI (Distributions to Paid-In) has been low for many funds . As a result, LPs have been reducing commitments to new funds or negotiating smaller fund sizes, and concentrating with GPs who have shown an ability to return capital even in tough times . GPs, on their side, are adapting by extending fund lives, considering secondary exits, or resizing fund targets. In boom times (e.g., 2021’s frenzy), LP–GP tensions are lower because paper gains and quick exits make everyone happy; in lean times, GPs must work harder to keep LPs onboard. Additionally, trends like ESG investing and diversity are influencing LP decisions – many institutional LPs now prefer or even require GPs to integrate environmental, social, and governance considerations, which adds another dimension to the partnership (mission alignment beyond just financial returns). What are emerging innovations in fund structure? The VC industry is experimenting with new models that impact LP–GP relations. Rolling funds (with quarterly subscriptions via platforms like AngelList) allow GPs to raise in a continuous way from a broad base of smaller LPs – this democratization means managing potentially hundreds of LPs and providing more frequent updates. Venture studios and corporate VCs are other models where the traditional LP–GP lines blur (corporate VCs invest off a balance sheet, for example, and venture studios start companies internally). Moreover, solo GP funds have risen (a single GP raising a fund, often leveraging personal brand and new tools), which can be attractive to certain LPs looking for niche strategies, but also concentrate key-person risk. These innovations require clear understanding with LPs about how the fund will operate. For example, a continuation fund (a GP-led secondary vehicle) might give existing LPs the choice to cash out or roll over – aligning with LP preferences is crucial to avoid any sense of GP self-dealing. The ILPA principles (from the Institutional Limited Partners Association) continue to evolve to provide best practices for these new contexts, emphasizing transparency and alignment in any new fund constructs.

## Optional Readings
Kauffman Fellows Report (2012). “We Have Met the Enemy… and He Is Us.” A famous study by the Kauffman Foundation (an active venture LP) analyzing two decades of their VC fund investments. It revealed that many VC funds underperformed public markets and that 75% of funds failed to return investors’ capital (net of fees) in line with risk taken . The report criticized misaligned incentives – such as large management fees allowing GPs to profit even without great returns – and too much capital in the industry chasing few winners. This optional read provides LP-side candor on structural issues in VC and suggests how LPs can push for better alignment (like smaller fund sizes, more GP commitment). It’s a bit dated and provocative, but it sparked improvements in LP due diligence and fund terms in the subsequent years. VenCap (2025). “The Return of the Power Law – VC Industry in 2025 and Beyond.” An investor letter by a fund-of-funds (VenCap) that gives a current overview of global VC market health and LP trends. It highlights how 2021’s boom led to a fundraising glut, followed by a sharp contraction – with 2024 seeing the lowest new VC funds in years as LPs grew cautious . Notably, it documents the “flight to quality” : a handful of large firms absorbed the bulk of LP capital, while nearly half of first-time funds from 2019 failed to raise a successor . The letter also connects the lack of exits to LP pullback, noting that with exits stuck, LPs prioritized re-investing with managers who did deliver some liquidity . This read offers a real-world LP perspective on recent market cycles and reinforces how intimately fund raising (LP–GP relations) is tied to exit outcomes and trust in manager skill.
## In-Class Activity
Fund Terms Negotiation Instructions: Students will be divided into pairs or small groups, with one side role-playing as a VC General Partner team raising a new fund and the other side as prospective Limited Partners (such as a university endowment or pension fund). Each pair is given a term sheet of key fund terms to negotiate: management fee %, carry %, GP commitment, fund size, investment focus, and any special provisions (e.g., co-investment rights for LPs, hurdle rate, fund term length). The GP group must propose terms that balance attractiveness to LPs with allowing them to operate effectively; the LP group has mandates to secure favorable terms and ensure alignment. The class will debrief, touching on how these negotiated terms impact the GP’s incentives and behavior throughout the fund’s life, and how LPs balance encouraging the GP (so they have resources and motivation to find great deals) with disciplining the GP (so they don’t earn outsized money without performing).
## Case Study
Evergreen Ventures – Managing a VC Fund in Turbulent Times Background: Evergreen Ventures is a mid-sized venture capital firm based in North America with two funds under management. Their first fund (Fund I, $150M) is 8 years old and has had some successes but few exits so far – it’s showing a TVPI (Total Value to Paid In) of 1.4× on paper, but a low DPI (Distributions to Paid In) of 0.2×, since only one moderate exit has occurred. Their second fund (Fund II, $200M) is 3 years in, still early in its investment period. Evergreen’s LPs are a mix of institutional investors (a college endowment, a pension fund) and family offices , many of whom invested in both funds. Now, Evergreen is thinking about raising a third fund, but macro conditions have been challenging – exits are slow, and LPs are concerned about over-commitment. Meanwhile, some portfolio stars in Fund I have grown huge (on paper) but haven’t exited (one is a unicorn still private). Situation: The managing partners of Evergreen Ventures face multiple dilemmas that test LP–GP relationships and fund management skills: - Fund I nearing its term: With Fund I in year 8, the LPA allows two one-year extensions. The GPs believe a couple of their big investments in Fund I (e.g., a fintech unicorn) could exit within 2–3 years if the IPO market improves. They want to extend the fund to maximize returns. However, one large LP in Fund I (a pension fund) is impatient and would prefer to wind down sooner, even if it means pushing for secondary sales of those stakes. This LP is vocal that they need distributions for their own cash flow. - Liquidity options: To appease LPs, Evergreen’s GPs are considering a GP-led secondary for Fund I’s prized asset: rolling that fintech unicorn and perhaps one other late-stage company into a new Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) – effectively a continuation fund. Interested Fund I LPs could roll into the SPV to stay for the eventual exit, while others could cash out (the secondary buyer would provide liquidity). This move requires 10 LP approval and delicate negotiation on pricing (what valuation for the asset on the secondary sale?). - Fundraising for Fund III: Evergreen has a decent reputation but not a top-tier brand. Given the soft environment, some of their usual LPs are hesitant to commit to a new fund without seeing more realized returns from Fund I. The college endowment LP communicates that they will commit only half of their previous amount to Fund III unless Evergreen brings in a strong exit or a new anchor LP. On the bright side, a new potential LP – a large tech corporate investor – is interested in Fund III but is pushing for special terms (e.g., a co-investment right to directly invest alongside the fund in deals, and a seat on the advisory committee). Evergreen’s partners must balance catering to this new LP’s requests with fairness to existing LPs and adherence to ILPA best practices. Data Provided: The case provides Fund I and II performance metrics (IRR, TVPI, DPI), a summary of key LPA terms (management fee, carry, extensions, etc.), and profiles of the major LPs with their concerns. It also details the big fintech unicorn: its last valuation, prospects, and the secondary market valuation offered by a reputable secondary fund (e.g., they offer to buy at a 20% discount to last round valuation). Emails from two LPs are shown: one from the impatient pension fund pressing for liquidity, and one from the new corporate LP outlining their desired terms for Fund III. Case Questions: 1. Extension vs. Secondary: Should Evergreen extend Fund I to wait for a full exit of the fintech unicorn, or proceed with the GP-led secondary for that asset? Consider the pros and cons of each from both GP and LP perspectives. (Extension: gives GPs more time to possibly achieve a great exit and higher carry, but LPs must wait longer and might prefer certainty now. Secondary: offers liquidity to willing LPs and potentially secures some carry for GPs if sold at a gain, but could be seen as selling the crown jewel at a discount and might upset LPs who wanted the big home-run.) What would you advise to maintain LP trust? 2. Managing LP communications: How should Evergreen communicate with Fund I LPs about the situation? Draft the key points for an LP letter or meeting where they propose their plan (be it extension or secondary), ensuring transparency about the rationale and how LP interests are considered. What LPAC approvals would be needed? How to address the pension fund’s urgency for cash versus others who may be more patient? 3. Fund III terms and LP negotiations: Evaluate the new corporate LP’s requests (co-invest rights and advisory seat). Are these reasonable asks in the current climate? How might granting those affect other LPs or the fund’s operation? (Co-invest rights can be positive to attract a big check, but other LPs may expect similar access or worry about conflicts; an advisory seat for an investor could be valuable expertise or could complicate decision-making if the corporate has strategic agendas.) Should Evergreen agree to these terms to secure the anchor commitment? If so, how can they structure it to remain fair (e.g., offer pro-rata co- invest opportunities to all large LPs, not just one)? 4. Maintaining alignment and trust: With Fund I underperforming on DPI, what can Evergreen’s GPs do to demonstrate alignment with LPs? (Think of actions like reducing or waiving management fees on an extension, or the GPs themselves participating in the secondary purchase to show conviction, etc.) How can they reassure LPs about Fund III’s prospects in light of Fund I’s delays? Identify at least two steps to rebuild or reinforce trust (for example, adjusting Fund III terms – maybe offering a slightly lower management fee or hurdle – to show they empathize with LPs’ low returns to date). 5. Long-term relationship considerations: If you were an LP in Evergreen Fund I and II, what factors would determine whether you invest in Fund III? How much weight do you put on past performance vs. forward-looking factors (like the quality of Evergreen’s pipeline or team)? This question puts students in the LP’s shoes to decide if Evergreen remains a worthy GP relationship despite some hiccups. Discuss what 11 Evergreen can leverage – perhaps Fund II’s early portfolio looks promising, or the team has learned from Fund I – in their fundraising narrative. Discussion: This case study discussion will surface the real tensions in LP–GP relations. Some groups might decide to extend Fund I and forgo an early liquidity event, prioritizing maximum value – but they must justify how to keep LPs on board (perhaps by offering fee concessions). Others might choose the secondary route to show responsiveness to LP liquidity needs . We’ll also hear varied approaches to handling the new LP: some will be eager to accommodate to get the fund raised, while others will be cautious about setting precedents. We’ll connect these choices to course principles: aligning interests (e.g., using hurdles or GP commit to reassure LPs), communication strategies in tough times, and the delicate balance of GP fiduciary duty (to maximize returns) vs. practical LP relationship management . By the end, students should appreciate that venture fund management involves not just picking startups but also skillful management of the investors who back the fund – a dual role of investor and client service that defines a venture capitalist’s responsibilities to their limited partners.
