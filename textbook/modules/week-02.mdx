**Source heading:** Week 2: Evaluating Venture Opportunities and Business Planning

## Topic Narrative
This week delves into how venture investors evaluate startup opportunities and how entrepreneurs craft business plans or pitches that align with those evaluation criteria. From a VC’s perspective, deciding which ventures to fund is a critical skill – it involves assessing the founder’s idea on multiple dimensions: the problem being solved, the size of the opportunity, the strength of the solution and business model, the competitive landscape, and the capabilities of the founding team. One useful framework introduced is the Venture Evaluation Matrix , which examines an opportunity across nine key factors: Need (is there a real customer pain point?), Solution (is the product or service compelling and innovative?), Team (do the founders have the skills and drive to execute?), Market (how large and growing is the target market?), Competition (who else is addressing this need and what is the startup’s edge?), Network (can the startup leverage partnerships or connections to accelerate growth?), Sales/Marketing (is there a clear path to acquire customers), Production/Development (can they deliver the product reliably at scale?), and Organization (are internal operations and structure sound for growth). A venture must be reasonably 11. 12. 9 10 11 strong across many of these dimensions to attract investment; a glaring weakness in one area can be a deal-breaker (e.g., a great idea in a tiny market, or a huge market but no credible plan to reach it). We also contrast traditional business plans with modern lean startup thinking. Classic business plans (detailed 30-page documents) outline the opportunity, strategy, financial projections, and risks. They force founders to think through every aspect of the business. Investors historically expected a polished plan, but today many VCs prefer a concise pitch deck and evidence of market traction over lengthy text. The lean startup approach – “build-measure-learn” – suggests that initial plans will evolve, and emphasizes testing assumptions with real customers rather than relying only on paper forecasts . However, whether via a plan or a pitch, founders still must tell a compelling story that covers the fundamentals: who is the customer, what problem is being solved, why now, how will you make money, and who will execute the plan. Investors typically pay closest attention to the management team and market size . A common saying is “Bet on the jockey, not just the horse” – a great team can adapt the business if needed . They also seek a large addressable market ; a startup targeting a billion-dollar market has more upside than one in a niche $20M market . During this week, students will learn how to perform a quick opportunity assessment: analyzing market research data, sizing the market (TAM/SAM/SOM methodology for total vs. attainable market), and identifying a venture’s unique value proposition . We will study examples of real startup pitches and discuss why some opportunities were deemed attractive and others weren’t. From the entrepreneur’s side, we cover the structure of a business plan and pitch deck . Key sections include: Executive Summary, Market/Opportunity, Product or Service Description, Business Model (how it will make money), Team & Organizational Plan, Marketing/Sales strategy, Competition analysis, Financial projections, and Risks. We emphasize that projections for startups are educated guesses; savvy founders acknowledge assumptions and have a plan to test them. Clarity and credibility are paramount – investors can spot overly optimistic claims or omitted challenges as red flags. By understanding how VCs evaluate opportunities, students will learn to refine business ideas and plans to address investors’ concerns. For instance, if a student were pitching a new venture, they should be prepared to answer: What evidence do you have of customer need? How big can this get? Why are you and your team the ones to do it? This week equips students with the analytical lenses to both critique venture opportunities and to assemble their own venture’s story in a way that passes an investor’s initial screening.
## Key Questions & Takeaways
What key factors do investors examine when evaluating a startup opportunity? **Answer:** Investors generally look at a combination of factors often summarized as the 4 M’s : Market (size and growth – is the opportunity big enough?), Model (business model – how will the startup make money and is it scalable?), Management (the founding team’s experience, skills, and track record), and Moat (competitive advantage – what protects this venture from competitors). Some frameworks break it down further (e.g., problem/solution fit, product, traction, financials, etc.), but fundamentally investors want a large and growing market, a compelling solution with a viable model, founders who can execute, and some edge or strategy against competition. If any one of these is very weak (say, a tiny market or an inexperienced, uncoachably poor team), the investment is likely a “pass.” How do venture investors size a market, and why is market size so important? **Answer:** Investors typically use concepts like Total Addressable Market (TAM) – the total annual revenue 11 12 12 13 1. 2. 12 if the startup achieved 100% market share in its defined field. They may estimate TAM through bottom-up analysis (e.g., number of potential customers × price per unit) or top-down (industry reports). They also consider Serviceable Available Market (SAM) – the portion of TAM the startup’s current product can serve, and Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM) – realistically, the share they could capture in the next few years. Market size is crucial because venture returns require scale; a startup in a huge market can potentially grow into a very valuable company, whereas even a perfectly executed business in a small market has an upper limit on value. As a rule of thumb, VCs often look for markets in the billions of dollars . A niche market might sustain a good small business, but not the “home run” outcome investors need. What makes a founding team attractive to investors? **Answer:** Investors look for a credible, coachable, and passionate team. “Credible” means the founders have relevant skills or industry experience (or have recruited advisors to fill gaps) – for example, a biotech startup led by a PhD in that scientific field plus a business-savvy co-founder. They like teams with a track record (e.g., previous startup successes or domain expertise) or at least some evidence of execution ability (a prototype built, early customers signed, etc.). “Coachability” is also cited: VCs avoid teams that seem arrogant or inflexible, since startups often need to pivot. Passion and commitment are important because startups are grueling; part-time or lukewarm founders are red flags. The composition of the team matters too – a balanced team (e.g., a technical lead + a sales/ marketing lead) often trumps a solo founder trying to do everything. In short, investors ask: ’Is this team capable of delivering on the vision, and will they persevere and adapt as needed?” If the answer is yes, that heavily weighs in the startup’s favor. Why are many investors today more interested in a pitch deck and traction than a lengthy business plan? **Answer:** In practice, investors have limited time and see hundreds of deals, so they prefer a concise pitch deck (10–15 slides) that hits the high points: problem, solution, market, team, business model, traction, and ask (how much money is being raised). A deck forces clarity and brevity. Additionally, the rise of the lean startup philosophy means investors expect that early plans will change; they place more weight on actual traction – real user or revenue growth, product prototypes, or other validation – than on theoretical five-year forecasts. A classic 40-page business plan is often seen as too rigid or filled with uncertain guesses, whereas a pitch deck paired with a demo or pilot results shows evidence of how the venture is actually performing or learning. That said, the planning process is still valuable – founders must know their strategy and have done homework – but the output is often delivered in a focused form. Some investors will request a written executive summary or a financial model spreadsheet after an initial pitch, but rarely the old-fashioned full business plan. The key takeaway: entrepreneurs need to show, not just tell – traction speaks louder than words, and clarity is preferred over quantity in documentation. How can the “lean startup” approach complement or conflict with traditional business planning? **Answer:** The lean startup approach encourages quick experimentation and iteration (e.g., releasing a minimum viable product, getting customer feedback, and pivoting if needed) rather than spending months writing a static plan. It complements traditional planning by providing real-world data to inform the plan – for instance, rather than guessing revenue in year 3, a lean startup might run a small pilot to gauge customer interest and use that data. However, it can conflict with traditional planning in that a business plan assumes a chosen direction, whereas lean methodology assumes 3. 4. 5. 13 the direction might change. Some investors worry that too little planning (just “figure it out as we go”) can lead to chaos or lack of strategy. The best approach is a hybrid : use lean tactics to test hypotheses early (so your plan is grounded in reality), but also maintain a strategic vision and roadmap. In class, we emphasize that while no plan survives first contact with customers, the process of planning – identifying assumptions and how to test them – is invaluable. Investors appreciate founders who are agile experimenters and thoughtful planners: they adjust course based on evidence but also have a clear end goal in mind (product-market fit and scaling). What common red flags might cause an investor to reject a business plan or pitch? **Answer:** There are several red flags that quickly sour investors: Unrealistic projections: e.g., claiming “\$100M revenue by Year 3” with no convincing basis, or huge market share assumptions (like “we just need 1% of China!”). Overly rosy numbers without justification signal naïveté or ignorance. Lack of competition statement: Saying “we have no competition” is almost always a red flag – it either means the founders haven’t done their research or there’s no market demand (if no one else is trying to solve it). Overemphasis on idea, underemphasis on execution: If a plan is heavy on concept but light on how the team will execute (e.g., no go-to-market strategy), investors worry the founders don’t know how to operationalize the idea. Team red flags: Missing key skills (no tech person for a software startup), high founder turnover, or unwillingness to seek advice are negatives. Also, if the cap table shows lots of equity already given away or co-founder disputes, that’s concerning. Not addressing risks: A pitch that ignores obvious risks (technical hurdles, regulatory issues, adoption challenges) can be a turn-off. Investors know every startup has risks; they want to see founders who acknowledge and have mitigation plans for those risks. Recognizing these red flags helps students understand how to avoid them in their own plans. Conversely, we highlight “green flags”: realistic assessment of market, evidence of early customer love, founders who know what they don’t know, etc. This sharpened lens will be applied when students critique sample pitches in class.

## In-Class Activity
Pitch Deck Critique Workshop Setup: Prior to class, students are given two sample startup pitch decks (each ~12 slides) – one from a hypothetical tech startup and one from a non-tech small business. In class, students form small groups. Each group is assigned one of the pitch decks to analyze from an investor’s perspective. Materials: Printed copies of the sample pitch decks or electronic access for each student; a checklist of evaluation criteria (team, market, product, business model, traction, etc.) is provided as a handout. Instructions: Review the Pitch: Groups spend 10 minutes reading through their assigned pitch deck, ensuring they catch every slide’s main points. Apply Investor Lens: Using the provided criteria checklist, groups discuss how the venture fares on each dimension. They identify strengths (e.g., “Founders have industry experience” or “Market is growing 30% annually”) and weaknesses/red flags (e.g., “No clear revenue model mentioned” or “Very optimistic sales projections without backing data”). They fill out brief notes for each criterion. Prepare Feedback: Each group formulates a brief feedback statement as if advising the startup or reporting to a partner meeting. This includes whether they (as investors) would be interested in a follow-up meeting or would pass, and the top 2–3 reasons why. They also come up with at least one question they’d ask the founders if they could (e.g., “How do you plan to acquire customers given competition from X?”). (~15 minutes for steps 2–3.) Presentation and Discussion: A spokesperson from each group presents their conclusions. For the tech startup pitch, multiple groups that reviewed it compare their impressions – did they all spot the same issues? For the non-tech pitch, the same comparison. The instructor writes on the board a tally of positives and negatives mentioned for each sample pitch. Facilitation: The instructor prompts groups to be specific. If a group says “Team slide was weak,” the instructor might ask “What was missing or concerning about it?” or “How could it be improved?”. During the discussion, the instructor can reveal any known outcome (if these are real cases) – e.g., “Interestingly, the tech startup failed to raise money, likely for the reasons you identified.” or “The non-tech business secured angel funding after tweaking their model.” This gives students closure and real-world validation of their analysis. Expected Outputs: Students practice the skill of critical evaluation . By the end, each student will have seen analyses of two pitches. The main output is a collective list of do’s and don’ts for venture pitches derived from the critique (the instructor can synthesize: e.g., Do highlight a specific customer problem; Don’t present unfounded financials; Do showcase some early traction, etc.). ## Case Study
InNovaTech or In-No-Vation? (Evaluating a Startup Opportunity) Case Background: Andrea Silva, a recent engineering grad, has developed a novel device called InNovaTech Sensor – a low-cost air quality sensor for home use that syncs with a smartphone. She founded InNovaTech, a startup aiming to tap into the growing consumer health and smart home market. Andrea has a prototype and early pilot results showing the sensor can reliably detect pollutants (like carbon monoxide, VOCs, etc.) and alert users via an app. She’s entering an accelerator and preparing to pitch angel investors. It’s mid-2025, and consumer awareness of indoor air quality is rising (especially after people spent more time at home in recent years). Andrea’s sensor could ride this trend. However, competition exists: a few established companies (e.g., Nest, now part of Google, has announced exploring air sensors in their devices) and several startups are working on similar technology. Andrea’s angle is that her sensor uses a new nano-material, giving it potentially better accuracy at lower cost. She’s filed a provisional patent on this. Andrea’s team is just herself and a part-time software developer she contracted to build the app. She’s seeking \$250,000 in seed funding to refine the product and finance a small initial production run. An angel syndicate has asked for a business plan and projections. Andrea, being more of a tech person, cobbled together a business plan which includes optimistic numbers – projecting that within 3 years, if all goes well, InNovaTech could sell 50,000 units at \$50 each, for \$2.5M revenue, and possibly reach \$10M in 5 years by expanding to international markets (Exhibit 1). She assumes a rapid adoption due to increased concerns about air health. However, she has not yet done a full competitive analysis or go-to-market strategy beyond “sell online direct to consumers and partner with home device retailers.” VC Associate Analysis: Enter Julian Lee, an associate at CleanTech Venture Partners, who is reviewing InNovaTech’s opportunity as a case study (the accelerator invited him as a mentor to give feedback). Julian uses a scoring sheet to evaluate startups like InNovaTech (Exhibit 2 shows a simplified version of his Venture Evaluation Matrix for InNovaTech). Here’s what he observes: Need/Problem: There is a clear problem – indoor air pollution can cause health issues, and consumers lack easy ways to monitor it. So, need is present, although one could argue it’s not as immediately felt as something like home security (air is invisible and risks are long-term). Solution/Product: Andrea’s prototype works in pilot tests. It’s innovative in that it’s smaller and cheaper than many industrial sensors. However, it’s still early – she hasn’t yet demonstrated it at scale or in varied home environments. The product’s value proposition is “peace of mind about the air you breathe” and early warning of dangers (like carbon monoxide leaks). That’s compelling, but consumer education may be needed to drive adoption. Market: The market size for smart home air quality devices is hard to pin down. Andrea’s plan claims a TAM of \$1 billion (her logic: X million households times an estimated \$Y willing to spend). Julian thinks the realistic market (SAM) might be much smaller initially – early adopters who are health-conscious or tech-savvy. It’s a growing niche, though, with potential to expand as awareness grows. The market growth tailwinds (post-pandemic health focus, smart home trend) are positive. He flags that if giants like Google fully enter, they could both expand and dominate the market.

Lecture Notes Opportunity Analysis Fundamentals: We begin by outlining how to break down a venture idea into analyzable pieces. Students learn the importance of clearly defining: What problem is being solved and for whom (target customer)? Why is the solution unique or better than existing alternatives? This forms the core of evaluating value proposition . We introduce the concept of product-market fit – the idea that a great innovation must align with a real market need. An example discussed: a startup making a high-tech smart fridge – we’d examine if customers actually want a smart fridge or if it’s innovation chasing a problem. The Investor’s Criteria Checklist: Using a slide or whiteboard, we enumerate typical criteria investors use. Common list: Team, Market, Product/Tech, Business Model, Competitive Advantage, Traction, Financials. We explain each: Team: Who are the founders and key team members? Why are they suited to tackle this venture (background, expertise, passion)? Investors often say they’d rather invest in an A-team with a B-idea than a B-team with an A-idea. We highlight examples of strong teams (e.g., serial entrepreneurs with prior successes) versus red flags (e.g., a solo founder trying to do everything in a complex business, unresolved co-founder conflicts, etc.). Market: How large is the opportunity in dollar terms? Is it growing? We might do a mini-exercise calculating TAM for a simple scenario (e.g., a new tutoring app – how many potential users x price, etc.). We also discuss market timing – is this the right time for this idea (too early, too late)? Example: Online grocery delivery was attempted in the 90s (Webvan) and failed, but later succeeded (Instacart) when technology and consumer habits caught up. Students learn to differentiate between a feature and a durable advantage. For example, “we have no competitors yet” is not an advantage (competitors can emerge), whereas “we have developed proprietary AI that is 2 years ahead of others” could be (assuming it’s true and protectable). We also discuss that often speed and execution can be an advantage – being the first to scale in a new market can confer brand and cost advantages that others find hard to overcome. Traction: Evidence of market demand or progress. Can be users, revenue, partnerships, successful pilot studies, etc. For early-stage startups, even modest traction (like 1,000 beta sign-ups or letters of intent) can significantly boost investor confidence, because it validates that “this solves a real problem for real people.” We underline that traction trumps theory: a live customer using a product provides more valuation uplift than a 50-page plan forecasting customers. Qualitative vs Quantitative Assessment: We highlight that evaluating ventures is both art and science. Quantitative aspects include market sizing, financial projections (revenue, burn rate, required capital). Qualitative aspects include founder passion, vision, and how well the story is told. Investors often talk about gut feeling or “founder-market fit” (does this founder have a deep personal insight or connection to the problem?). We caution that while gut feel is real, our goal is to base assessments on evidence as much as possible. Tools like scorecards or matrices (like Hellmann’s Venture Evaluation Matrix) can help make the process systematic, but there’s always subjective judgment. We might show a simple scoring example: scoring two hypothetical pitches on a 1–5 scale across key factors to see which looks stronger – illustrating how a weaker team or tiny market can drag down the overall attractiveness. Business Plan vs. Lean Canvas: We compare the traditional business plan format to the modern lean canvas (a one-page template capturing key elements: customer problem, solution, unique value, metrics, channels, etc.). The class discusses pros and cons: Business plans are great for forcing detailed thinking (and some investors in certain industries, like biotech or banking, still expect them), but they can become quickly outdated. Lean canvases and pitch decks are concise and iterate- friendly, but risk oversimplifying (e.g., not including any financial projections initially). Action Point: Students will actually practice writing a one-page opportunity summary (like an elevator pitch or executive summary) this week, distilling their venture idea or a case study venture into the core elements. This builds the skill of succinctly communicating an opportunity.
