## Topic Narrative
A capitalization table (cap table) is a ledger of a company’s ownership: it lists all equity holders (founders, investors, option pools) and their shares/percentages . It is the single source of truth for “who owns what” at any point in time. Maintaining a clear cap table is crucial for understanding dilution – how each financing round or option grant affects existing owners’ percentages . Early on, a cap table might be as simple as three founders owning 100% between them. But as the startup raises money and issues options to employees, the cap table evolves into a complex, multi-layered structure with common stock, preferred stock (Series A, B, etc.), and pools for options/warrants . When a startup raises a new round, new shares are issued which dilute (reduce the percentage ownership of) the existing shareholders. For example, consider a company with 1,000,000 shares owned by founders (100%). If an investor puts in money and is issued 250,000 new shares, the company now has 1,250,000 shares – the founders still have their 1,000,000 shares but now own 80% (1M/1.25M) post-financing. This dilution is a natural part of growth – founders trade some ownership for the capital to grow the pie. A cap table before and after the financing would clearly show this: e.g. Pre-round – Founder A 50%, B 30%, C 20%. Post-round – maybe Founders A/B/C go down proportionally to 40/24/16% (assuming no other changes) and the new investor takes 20%. Key metrics on the cap table are often considered on a fully diluted basis , meaning assuming all outstanding convertible securities (options, warrants, SAFEs, etc.) are converted to common shares . This gives the true eventual ownership if everyone exercises their rights. For instance, a startup might say “Investor X owns 20% on a fully diluted basis,” taking into account the employee stock options that will be issued. It’s important to model dilution not just in the current round but across future rounds (hence VCs often construct multi-round cap table models). Founders should anticipate how their slice may shrink over time with multiple raises. One concept is the option pool shuffle : often investors require the company to create or top-up an employee option pool as part of a financing, and critically, they want this done pre-money – i.e. before calculating their ownership. This effectively dilutes the founders, not the new investor. For example, if an investor wants a 10% option pool post-round, and they’re investing such that they’ll own 20%, the pool typically comes out of the remaining founder percentage. Founders need to account for this when negotiating valuation (a “\$10M pre-money with 10% pool” is roughly equivalent to \$9M pre without a pool, since creating the pool dilutes existing shares). Cap tables also reflect different classes of shares. Preferred shares (held by VCs) have preferences as per term sheets, whereas common shares are held by founders and employees. When analyzing dilution, one 32 41 42 43 44 45 46 10 looks at conversion to common – e.g., preferred shares typically convert to common on an IPO or major exit, so the cap table often shows an “as-converted” basis so one can see ultimate ownership . Managing the cap table involves scenario planning. For instance, if the company raises another round, how does that dilute everyone? Founders and early investors can use cap table models to answer questions like: “If we raise \$5M at a \$20M pre-money next year, what will everyone’s ownership look like?” It also helps highlight issues like founder dilution after many rounds. A famous scenario: a founder starts at 100%, after a Seed, Series A, B, etc., they might end up below 20%. Founders must balance bringing in capital with maintaining a significant stake. Anti-dilution provisions (from Week 6) can also affect the cap table: in a down round, if earlier investors get more shares to compensate, that comes at the expense of founders and possibly other investors. This will show up on the cap table as an issuance of extra shares to the protected investors, further diluting others. Another critical use of the cap table is to understand who has control . Beyond just percentages, the cap table combined with voting rights will show if founders still have majority voting power or if investors as a group have taken the lead. For example, after several rounds, investors might collectively own 60%+ of the company – meaning common shareholders (founders/employees) are a minority. This has implications for decision-making and exit approval (if investors hold a majority of votes, they can approve a sale even if founders disagree, depending on voting rights). Cap tables can also become messy with many SAFE or convertible note conversions. When those convert at a priced round, suddenly a large number of shares may be issued to note holders, causing unexpected dilution if not planned. It’s crucial to keep an eye on these “cap table time bombs.” A classic mistake is not accounting for the shares that will be created by a conversion of, say, \$1M in SAFEs with a low valuation cap – which could significantly dilute the founders at Series A closing. A practical skill is cap table modeling : using a simple spreadsheet to input the pre-money valuation, the new money, any pool increase, etc., and let it calculate the new ownership structure . Students and entrepreneurs should practice building these models to avoid surprises. Modern cap table management tools (Carta, Capshare, etc.) are often used by startups to track this dynamically . In summary, cap tables tell the story of the company’s ownership over time. Dilution is inevitable as a startup raises capital, but it should come with an increase in company value so that everyone’s piece of the pie, while smaller percentage-wise, is hopefully worth more in absolute terms. An old rule of thumb: “I’d rather own 10% of a billion-dollar company than 100% of nothing.” Understanding cap tables ensures that founders know how that 10% might come to be, and to manage their equity intelligently through many financing events.
## Key Questions & Takeaways
What is a cap table and why is it important? – It’s a spreadsheet or table that lists all shareholders and their ownership (shares and percentages) . It shows the structure of equity at any time. It’s important because it tracks dilution, rights, and ownership – helping founders, investors, and employees understand their stake. A well-maintained cap table is needed for fundraising (investors will examine it), decision-making (who has voting control), and compliance (e.g. stock option allocations) . 45 47 48 42 1. 41 43 49 11 How does a financing round dilute existing shareholders? – When new shares are issued to a new investor, existing owners’ percentage ownership decreases because the total share count increases. For example, if you owned 100% of a company and then bring in an investor who will own 25%, your share goes to 75% (100% × (old shares/ new total shares)). The cap table will show pre- vs. post-round ownership. Importantly, dilution is not inherently bad if the investment raises the company’s value – often everyone’s value goes up even as percentages go down. What does “fully diluted” mean? – ’Fully diluted” means counting all possible shares if every option, warrant, SAFE, etc., is converted/exercised . It’s the scenario of maximum share count. Startups use fully diluted shares to give a realistic percentage ownership. For instance, if a company has 1M shares outstanding but also an option pool of 200k unissued options and a note that would convert into 100k shares, the fully diluted share count is 1.3M. So an investor owning 130k shares is 10% on a fully diluted basis (130k/1.3M), even if currently those 100k note shares aren’t yet issued. How do option pools affect the cap table? – Employee option pools are typically set aside as a percentage of the company for current/future hires. If an option pool is increased, it effectively creates new potential shares, diluting existing holders. Investors often demand a certain pool size post-deal (say 10– 15%). Critically, they usually want the pool created pre-money , meaning the dilution primarily hits the founders. For example, a term sheet might say ’\$X investment at \$Y pre-money with a 10% post-money option pool.” This means before the investment closes, the company must add shares to ensure 10% of the post-money is unallocated options – reducing founder percentages. What is a pro forma cap table and why model future rounds? – A pro forma cap table is one that models the structure after a proposed transaction (financing or exit). Startups create pro formas for future rounds to see dilution impact. Modeling, for example, raising \$10M in Series B at a certain valuation will show founders how their ~\% might drop from, say, 30% to 20%. It’s important because founders can plan – e.g., maybe grant fewer options now if they know a big pool increase is needed later, or realize that after Series C they’ll fall below a control threshold. VC funds also model pro formas to see what ownership they’ll end up with if they follow-on invest in later rounds, etc. In short, projecting the cap table forward helps stakeholders make informed decisions about financing strategy. What happens on an exit? (Cap table perspective) – The cap table, combined with liquidation preferences, determines the payout. In a simple scenario, if the company is sold, proceeds are split by ownership. But if there are liquidation preferences, the cap table must be overlaid with those terms. For example, if VCs have a \$10M preference, they take that off the top before common shareholders (founders/employees) get anything. One can do a waterfall analysis based on the cap table: simulate a sale price and allocate who gets what. Understanding the cap table is thus key to knowing how much each stakeholder would receive at various exit values.

## Case Study
Scaling and Dilution at UrbanGardens Background: UrbanGardens is a hardware-enabled startup (IoT sensors for urban farming) that has gone through Seed and Series A rounds. The case chronicles their financing history and cap table evolution: - At start: 2 founders, one early employee. Founders owned 90% (split 50/40) and the early employee 10%. - Seed: An angel syndicate invested \$500k on a \$4.5M pre-money (so \$5M post). They received Series Seed preferred representing 10% ownership post-deal. The company created a 5% option pool pre-money for future hires. The case provides the cap table after Seed: e.g. Founders now combined ~76.5%, employee 8.5%, new angel investors 10%, option pool 5%. (Founders got diluted from 90% to ~76.5%, etc.) - Series **Answer:** One year later, a VC leads a \$3M Series A at \$15M pre-money. By now, some options were granted from the pool to a few new employees. The Series A deal requires expanding the option pool to 10% post-round. The Series A VC gets, say, 20% ownership post-round. The case shows the complicated cap table calculations: the pool top-up was done pre-money, meaning existing holders (founders, seed investors, etc.) were diluted even before the new money. After Series A, maybe Founders end up at 50% combined, Seed investors 8%, Team (incl. granted options) 10%, Option pool unallocated 10%, Series A VC 20%, etc. The exact numbers are given in a table for students to analyze. 14 By Year 3, UrbanGardens is doing well but needs a Series B to fuel growth. Founders are grappling with the prospect of owning less than half of the company after Series B. They are also concerned about one founder’s stake dropping below 25%, a psychological threshold, and how later dilution might affect control (though they have voting agreements in place). Dilemma: Series B term sheets are coming in. The best offer is \$10M on a \$40M pre-money (so 20% to new investors). However, the VCs demand a refresh of the option pool to 10% again. Founders must decide how much dilution to accept. One founder considers not raising and growing slower to avoid dilution. The other argues that 30% of a big company is better than 50% of a small one. They also consider perhaps a slightly smaller raise (\$7M instead of \$10M) to retain more ownership, balancing growth needs vs. dilution.

But if a pool increase to 10% is required, that pool likely comes from pre-money – meaning effectively existing stakeholders will give up some percentage to create that. They’ll derive founders’ new % maybe dropping from ~50% to around 40%, earlier investors from 8% to ~6.5%, etc. This math solidifies their dilution modeling skills.) - Q3: What is the founders’ ownership likely after a future Series B and possibly Series C? Is their concern about going below 50% valid? (Students will discuss that losing majority can affect control, but many startups end up with founders below 50%. The key is whether they have other control mechanisms like board control or dual-class shares – not in this case, presumably. The psychological “50%” isn’t as important legally if voting is one-share-one-vote; once founders are below ~33%, they can even be outvoted if all investors align. It’s a chance to discuss control versus ownership.) - Q4: Should the founders consider raising less money to reduce dilution? What are the trade-offs? (Raising less might mean retaining a few more percentage points, but could starve growth or result in needing another fundraise sooner – which in turn causes dilution anyway. Raising more money can sometimes save dilution if it avoids an extra round. This question gets at strategic fundraising: sometimes taking a bit more dilution now to hit a big milestone can prevent a worse down round later. Students might bring up the concept of funding enough to reach cash-flow breakeven versus continual dilution.) - Q5: How does the option pool factor into their dilution, and what strategies can founders use regarding option pools? (Founders might negotiate to size the pool precisely to hiring needs so as not to over-dilute. They could also push to count the pool as part of the post-money (so investors share the dilution). Some might mention rolling unallocated pool from last round into the next rather than refreshing fully. The strategy is to not agree to an excessively large pool “just in case,” as that’s a big hit to founders.) Analysis: This case forces students to apply cap table math in a real context and consider the human side of dilution. Founders often feel anxiety as their ownership falls. The discussion should reinforce that while percentage ownership is important, what ultimately matters is the value of that ownership and maintaining enough control to steer the company. If UrbanGardens grows 10×, the founders’ smaller slice could be worth far more in absolute terms. Students will also see how successive option pool increases can significantly erode founder stakes if not managed. The instructor can share real-world outcomes (e.g., many 15 famous startups had founders owning <20% at IPO and still became millionaires, and remained key decision-makers due to board influence or dual-class shares). The case wraps up the section by underlining that dilution is a normal part of venture financing – not to be feared if managed and understood, but certainly to be modeled and strategized.
