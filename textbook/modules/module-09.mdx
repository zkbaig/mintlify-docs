## Key Concepts

- **Initial public offering (IPO):** Public listing for liquidity and capital, with regulatory burdens.
- **Mergers and acquisitions (M&A) exit:** Acquisition by another company; most common VC outcome.
- **Secondary sale:** Private share sale for partial liquidity.
- **Continuation fund:** Vehicle to hold assets longer when IPO windows are closed.
- **Drag-along rights:** Governance mechanism to align exit decisions.
- **Internal rate of return (IRR):** Annualized return metric used to compare investment performance.

## Main Content

### Chapter 1: Definitions and exit routes

Exits convert **paper value** into **liquid returns** and require deliberate trade-offs between speed, certainty, control, and upside. An IPO can maximize valuation and provide long-term capital access, but it introduces regulatory burden, market volatility, and staged liquidity. An acquisition typically provides faster, more certain cash proceeds, yet it can reduce strategic independence and long-term upside. A secondary sale offers partial liquidity without a full exit, but it can signal mixed commitment and complicate cap table alignment.

- **IPO:** Public listing for liquidity and capital access, with regulatory and market-execution risk.
- **Acquisition:** Strategic or financial buyer purchases company; offers speed and certainty at the cost of independence.
- **Secondary sale:** Selling shares to new investors before a full exit; provides interim liquidity but leaves residual risk.

<Callout type="note" title="Exit readiness">
Exits are not just events; they are planned outcomes aligned with cap table and governance.
</Callout>

#### Exit readiness checklist (governance, metrics, legal prep)

Use this checklist before committing to a process so the board can compare readiness across exit routes.

**Governance readiness**
- Board composition aligned to public-company standards (independent directors, audit committee).
- Drag-along and ROFR/Co-sale rights mapped with clear execution playbooks.
- Investor consent thresholds and required approvals documented.

**Metrics readiness**
- Predictable revenue quality (ARR retention, churn, gross margin stability).
- Unit economics and growth efficiency (LTV/CAC, payback period, Rule of 40).
- Forecast accuracy with clean KPI definitions (GAAP vs. non-GAAP adjustments).

**Legal + compliance readiness**
- Audited financials and up-to-date cap table (no unresolved option grants).
- IP assignment, data privacy, and key contracts vetted for change-of-control clauses.
- Litigation, regulatory, and employee classification risks assessed and remediated.

### Chapter 4: Worked example with numbers

The waterfall example below shows why exit values must be evaluated on a **proceeds** basis, not a headline valuation. It uses a simple acquisition scenario to illustrate how liquidation preferences and ownership percentages translate into real dollars for each stakeholder group.

**Scenario:** \$40M acquisition, 1× participating preference, Series A owns 30%. Use the waterfall below to see how preferences change the headline outcome.

| Step | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Preference | \$10M investment returned | \$10M to Series A |
| Remainder | \$40M - \$10M | \$30M |
| Ownership split | 30% of \$30M | \$9M to Series A |
| Total to Series A | \$10M + \$9M | \$19M |

**Takeaway:** A \$40M headline exit can translate to less than half of proceeds for common holders once preferences are applied.

In practice, this means boards must weigh exit options against **distribution outcomes** and **timing**. A lower-priced acquisition might deliver higher certainty and faster distributions, while a higher-priced IPO can still result in delayed or volatile liquidity because of lock-ups, market swings, and post-IPO performance risk.

#### Waterfall model comparison across exit types

**Assumptions:** \$20M invested preferred with 1× non-participating, 40% common, 60% preferred. Compare three exit outcomes.

| Exit path | Headline value | Preference payout | Remainder split (60/40) | Total to preferred | Total to common | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| IPO | \$300M | \$20M | \$280M | \$20M + \$168M = \$188M | \$112M | Liquidity staged by lock-up and market volatility. |
| M&A | \$220M | \$20M | \$200M | \$20M + \$120M = \$140M | \$80M | Clean cash close, faster distribution. |
| Secondary | \$150M | \$20M | \$130M | \$20M + \$78M = \$98M | \$52M | Partial liquidity; remaining equity still at risk. |

### Scenario exercise: Board decision under liquidity pressure

**Situation:** The company has 18 months of runway, the lead VC's fund has 2 years left, and founders want to stay independent. A strategic buyer offers \$260M cash now; IPO bankers project \$350M–\$450M in 12–18 months with market risk.

**Stakeholder incentives**
- **Founders:** Preserve control, maximize long-term impact, avoid rushed deal terms.
- **Employees:** Desire partial liquidity and retention packages; worry about layoffs in M&A.
- **VCs:** Need near-term DPI; prefer certainty to avoid fund extension.
- **Strategic buyer:** Wants quick close, IP lock-in, and lower integration risk.
- **Secondary investors:** Seek liquidity now but still want upside if IPO succeeds.

**Prompt:** As the board, choose an exit path and list the top three mitigations for the losing stakeholder group.

## Worked Examples

The following worked examples are designed as structured guidance. Each item shows how to move from an exit decision question to a concrete deliverable, emphasizing the analysis steps and the trade-offs that should be documented.

### Exit memo template (structured)

Include the following required sections in a 1–2 page memo:

1. **Executive summary** (recommended path + timing).
2. **Company readiness** (governance, metrics, legal).
3. **Market context** (IPO window, M&A activity, comps).
4. **Stakeholder incentives** (founders, employees, VCs, LPs).
5. **Waterfall outcomes** (base, downside, upside scenarios).
6. **Risks + mitigations** (market, regulatory, integration, retention).
7. **Recommendation + next steps** (timeline, advisors, diligence plan).

### Mini-exercise: Exit memo checklist

Use this mini-exercise to validate that your memo covers the minimum decision inputs. Start by drafting a one-page memo, then verify each category below with specific evidence or assumptions:

- **Market:** IPO window (public comps, volatility) and acquisition appetite (recent strategic deals).
- **Fund age:** Vintage year, remaining fund life, and distribution pressure.
- **Liquidity needs:** Founder/employee secondary interest and LP cash-return timing.

### In-class activity: Exit strategy simulation

This simulation converts exit theory into a boardroom decision process. Participants should treat it as a formal recommendation exercise with explicit assumptions and trade-offs.

- **Task:** VC board teams choose IPO, acquisition, or staying private longer.
- **Inputs:** Startup profile, cap table, macro environment, and LP memo.
- **Outcome:** Present rationale and risk trade-offs; compare across teams.

### Case study: SafeSpot's dilemma

Use this case study to practice balancing valuation, liquidity timing, and stakeholder incentives. The data below is intentionally concise so that teams must articulate assumptions about trade-offs.

- **Company:** Cybersecurity startup with \$60M annual recurring revenue (ARR), Series C at \$500M post.
- **Options:** IPO at \$800M–\$1B; acquisition at \$750M cash; secondary at \$600M.
- **Stakeholders:** VCs own 55%, founders/employees 35%, strategic investor 10%.
- **Decision factors:** IPO fees/volatility vs. acquisition certainty; fund distributions to paid-in capital (DPI) pressure.

### Discussion prompts and teaching notes

Treat the prompts below as a facilitation guide. Each prompt is paired with a teaching note to reinforce the specific trade-off or decision criterion students should surface.

- **Compare SafeSpot's acquisition vs. IPO outcomes.**
  - Teaching note: Consider fees, lock-ups, and risk-adjusted value.
- **How should the board evaluate IPO timing risk?**
  - Teaching note: Weigh certainty today vs. speculative upside.
- **What strategic factors beyond price matter?**
  - Teaching note: Independence, culture, and long-term growth.
- **How do stakeholder incentives differ?**
  - Teaching note: Fund DPI pressure vs. founder prestige and team retention.
- **Is a secondary a good middle path?**
  - Teaching note: Provides liquidity but may delay decision and dilute leverage.

## Practice Questions (with answers)

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="1) What is the primary governance readiness signal for IPOs?">
    **Answer:** Independent board oversight and audit committee readiness, plus documented approvals and controls.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="2) In a 1× non-participating preference, when does preferred convert?">
    **Answer:** When the conversion value exceeds the preference return, typically at higher exit values.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="3) Why might a secondary be favored during a closed IPO window?">
    **Answer:** It provides partial liquidity without the risks and timing uncertainty of public markets.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="4) Which stakeholder is most sensitive to DPI timing?">
    **Answer:** VCs approaching the end of a fund life.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="5) Name two metrics that strengthen IPO readiness.">
    **Answer:** Net revenue retention and gross margin stability (or Rule of 40 performance).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="6) What is a key downside of M&A versus IPO?">
    **Answer:** Reduced upside potential and loss of independence post-close.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="7) What is the biggest legal risk that delays exits?">
    **Answer:** Unresolved IP ownership or material litigation that scares buyers or underwriters.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="8) How can boards protect employees in an acquisition?">
    **Answer:** Negotiate retention packages, clear integration plans, and communication timelines.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
