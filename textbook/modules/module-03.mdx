## Key Concepts

- **Burn rate:** Net monthly cash outflow for a startup.
- **Runway:** Months of cash remaining at current burn rate.
- **Unit economics:** Revenue and cost per customer/unit, including margin and CAC/LTV.
- **CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost):** Cost to acquire a customer.
- **LTV (Lifetime Value):** Net revenue from a customer over time.
- **MRR (Monthly Recurring Revenue):** Recurring subscription revenue per month.
- **Churn:** Percentage of customers or revenue lost over a period.
- **Scenario analysis:** Base, upside, and downside forecasting to stress uncertainty.
- **Software-as-a-service (SaaS):** Subscription software delivered over the internet.
- **Average revenue per user (ARPU):** Revenue generated per user or account.
- **Cost of goods sold (COGS):** Direct costs required to deliver a product or service.
- **Business-to-business (B2B):** Sales to other businesses rather than consumers.
- **Accounts receivable (AR):** Amounts owed to the company by customers.
- **Accounts payable (AP):** Amounts the company owes to suppliers.
- **Research and development (R&D):** Product and technology development expenses.

## Main Content

### Chapter 1: Definitions and financial building blocks

Financial projections translate strategy into **quantified assumptions, timing, and cash implications**. Treat them as decision tools, not vanity charts: they exist to answer “Can we survive long enough to hit the next milestone?” and “What must be true for this plan to work?”

- **Income statement:** Revenue, expenses, and profitability.
- **Cash flow:** Actual cash movement; determines survival.
- **Burn rate:** Monthly net cash outflow.
- **Runway:** Cash ÷ burn rate.

<Callout type="warning" title="Cash reality">
A profitable income statement can still hide a cash crisis.
</Callout>

#### Cash vs. profitability (clarification)

Profit measures **earned revenue minus expenses**, while cash flow reflects **the timing of actual cash movements**.

**Concise example:** A startup books **\$120k** in annual subscriptions in January, but customers pay quarterly. It shows **\$10k/month** in revenue, but only **\$30k** cash comes in each quarter. If monthly expenses are **\$50k**, the company reports **\$-40k** operating loss in January yet still sees **negative cash flow** even as revenue grows, because cash collections lag expenses. The runway shrinks despite top-line growth.

### Chapter 2: Frameworks and decision tools

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Driver-based model">

#### Modeling from drivers

Use a short list of measurable drivers to build output lines. Keep the linkage explicit and consistent:

- **Leads → Paid customers:** Leads × conversion rate → New customers.
- **Customers → MRR:** Active customers × price per month → MRR.
- **MRR → Gross profit:** MRR × gross margin % → Gross profit.
- **Gross profit → Net burn:** Gross profit − operating expenses → Net burn.
- **Net burn → Runway:** Cash balance ÷ net burn → Months of runway.

**Simple unit economics template**

| Metric | Formula | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ARPU | Monthly revenue ÷ active customers | \$40 |
| Gross margin | (Revenue − COGS) ÷ Revenue | 75% |
| LTV | ARPU × gross margin ÷ churn | \$600 |
| CAC | Sales + marketing spend ÷ new customers | \$200 |
| LTV/CAC | LTV ÷ CAC | 3.0× |
| CAC payback | CAC ÷ (ARPU × gross margin) | 6.7 months |

### Chapter 4: Worked example with numbers

**Scenario:** 12-month plan for a B2B SaaS startup.

Purpose: the mini table below introduces how **MRR (Monthly Recurring Revenue)** growth translates into **net burn** before you extend the logic into a full cash runway model.

| Month | MRR | COGS (20%) | Opex | Net Burn |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | \$10k | \$2k | \$60k | -\$52k |
| 6 | \$40k | \$8k | \$75k | -\$43k |
| 12 | \$90k | \$18k | \$95k | -\$23k |

**Runway check:** \$900k cash ÷ \$50k avg burn ≈ **18 months**.

Takeaway: even modest MRR gains can materially lengthen runway when fixed costs remain stable.

#### Scenario expansion: base, downside, upside

Purpose: the scenario table formalizes **scenario analysis** by holding the model structure constant and varying only the key assumptions (growth, churn, CAC, and operating expenses).

| Scenario | Assumptions (12 months) | Summary outcomes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Base** | MRR grows **10%/mo**, churn **3%**, CAC **\$400**, opex grows **5%/qtr** | Month 12 MRR **\$90k**, avg burn **\$45k**, runway **~18 months** |
| **Downside** | MRR grows **6%/mo**, churn **6%**, CAC **\$550**, opex grows **8%/qtr** | Month 12 MRR **\$60k**, avg burn **\$60k**, runway **~12 months** |
| **Upside** | MRR grows **14%/mo**, churn **2%**, CAC **\$350**, opex grows **4%/qtr** | Month 12 MRR **\$115k**, avg burn **\$35k**, runway **~22 months** |

**Interpretation:** The downside case is driven by churn and CAC pressure that slow net growth and lift spend. If the downside still kills runway, you can’t raise yet—you need to fix retention, pricing, or spend before the next round. If the base case only barely makes it, you are planning to miss.

Takeaway: scenario analysis exposes how small changes in churn, CAC, and expense growth compound into materially different runway outcomes—and whether the plan survives contact with reality.

## Worked Examples

### Sample projection walkthrough

Purpose: this walkthrough demonstrates how to construct a projection that links revenue growth, operating expenses, and cash balance—and how to use it to decide when you actually need to raise.

- Build a multi-year or monthly projection that explicitly tracks revenue growth, operating expenses, and cash balance.
- Highlight the slow early revenue ramp and the rising staff, marketing, and research and development (R&D) costs that typically precede scale.
- Use the projection to show when additional funding is required and why the timing matters.
- Contrast startup financials, where losses are expected, with mature-company metrics to clarify stage-appropriate performance.

Takeaway: a structured projection clarifies funding timing and makes early-stage losses interpretable against growth expectations, so you can decide whether to slow spend, raise, or wait.

### In-class activity: Runway and unit economics simulation

<Callout type="info" title="Scenario snapshot: AlphaApp">
A mobile app startup has **\$200k** cash, **\$30k** monthly expenses, and **\$10k** monthly revenue. CAC is **\$10/user**, revenue is **\$2/user/month**, and users grow **10% monthly** from a base of 5,000.
</Callout>

#### Goals

- Practice runway calculations and visualize how burn changes as costs and revenue scale.
- Evaluate unit economics (CAC, LTV) and decide whether to raise now or fix the model first.

#### Steps

1. **Calculate runway:** Estimate net burn and months of runway today.
2. **Project cost growth:** Add \$5k/month in expenses for hiring or infrastructure and recalc runway at months 6 and 12.
3. **Check unit economics:** Compute CAC payback and LTV (use churn assumptions provided by the instructor).
4. **Make a funding call:** Decide whether to raise now or delay to hit stronger metrics.
5. **Share back:** Each group presents its runway, funding choice, and one adjustment to improve economics.

#### Debrief prompts

- What assumption (price, churn, CAC) changes runway the most?
- How do you reconcile “good” LTV/CAC with a short runway?

### Case study: FinFix Co. — financial planning for a fintech startup

#### Background

FinFix offers a freemium personal finance app with a **\$5/month** premium tier. The current scale is **100,000 users**, **5,000 paid** (5% conversion), growing primarily via word-of-mouth.

#### Current financials

Purpose: these snapshots establish the baseline MRR and burn profile that you will stress-test in the growth plan.

- **Revenue:** ~\$30k/month (subscriptions and referral commissions).
- **Expenses:** ~\$80k/month (payroll, servers, marketing, overhead).
- **Net burn:** ~\$50k/month with **~\$500k cash** remaining (~10 months of runway).

Takeaway: the baseline indicates a stable but time-limited runway that requires disciplined growth assumptions.

#### Growth plan assumptions (next 12 months)

- Add **20k users/month** (best case), or **10k/month** (downside).
- Lift conversion from **5% → 8%** with new premium features.
- Increase referral income to **\$15k/month** with deeper bank partnerships.

#### Projection highlights (Exhibit 1 summary)

- **Month 6:** ~180k users, ~\$70k MRR, ~\$110k expenses → ~\$40k burn.
- **Month 12:** ~300k users, ~\$135k MRR, ~\$140k expenses → near breakeven.
- **Funding gap:** Plan implies ~**\$1M** total annual burn; requires **\$500k** additional capital.

Takeaway: the growth plan closes the burn gap by month 12 but still requires interim financing.

#### Unit economics snapshot (Exhibit 2 summary)

- **CAC (blended):** ~\$2/user today; could rise with paid marketing.
- **Premium LTV:** ~\$100–\$150 (2–3 year lifetime estimate).
- **Conversion:** 5% now, target 8%; LTV:CAC for premium users ~3:1.
- **Burn multiple:** Improves from ~5:1 early to ~1:1 by year-end if growth holds.

Takeaway: the story only works if CAC discipline holds and conversion lift offsets the higher burn required to scale.

#### Investor perspective

Investors will test whether growth assumptions are plausible and whether CAC stays stable as spend scales. Key health signals include the paid conversion trend, churn, and recurring revenue momentum.

#### Decision point

Decision framing: raise in ~6 months at stronger metrics, or conserve cash and grow slower. Stress-test sensitivity by asking what happens if conversion only reaches **6%** or CAC doubles—if either breaks the runway, the plan is not fundable yet.

## Pitfalls

<Callout type="warning" title="Pitfalls to Avoid">
- Overestimating revenue growth without a go-to-market plan.
- Underestimating expenses, especially CAC, hiring, and overhead.
- Ignoring cash flow timing (revenue vs. cash receipts).
- Building projections with zero buffer for delays or overruns.
- Assuming profitability too early or ignoring a path to profitability entirely.
- Ignoring churn or assuming it “stays low” without evidence.
- Treating CAC as flat while scaling paid acquisition.
- Modeling perfect conversion lift without testing or cohort data.
- Using a single “hero” channel with no backup plan.
</Callout>

### Mini-exercise: Sensitivity pivot

Pick one pivot (churn or CAC) and estimate how runway changes.

1. **Baseline:** Assume 12 months of runway at today’s burn.
2. **Pivot 1 (churn):** Double churn (e.g., 4% → 8%) and reduce MRR growth by 30%. Estimate the new burn and runway.
3. **Pivot 2 (CAC):** Increase CAC by 50% and add the incremental monthly spend to operating expenses. Estimate runway again.
4. **Decision:** Which pivot is more damaging, and what single driver would you change first?

### Sensitivity lab: churn, CAC, or pricing

Pick one lever and quantify runway impact.

1. **Choose a baseline:** 12 months runway, \$50k net burn, \$80 ARPU, 4% churn, \$400 CAC.
2. **Run a sensitivity:**  
   - **Churn:** increase to 7% and reduce net MRR growth by 25%.  
   - **CAC:** increase to \$550 and add the incremental spend to opex.  
   - **Pricing:** increase ARPU by 10% with no churn change.  
3. **Interpret:** Identify which lever changes runway the most and explain why.

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Sample interpretation">
    **Answer:** Higher churn usually shortens runway most because it lowers retained revenue and slows compounding, while CAC spikes mainly add immediate burn. A pricing lift can offset modest CAC increases if churn stays flat.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

- **Why are projections valuable if they are inaccurate?** They surface assumptions, guide planning, and align fundraising to milestones.
- **What is burn rate and why does it matter?** It determines runway and survival; investors watch it closely.
- **Which unit economics matter most for startups?** Gross margin, CAC, LTV, ARPU, and churn depending on the model.
- **How should startups decide how much to raise?** Fund the next milestone plus a buffer, typically 12–18 months of runway.
- **What are common projection mistakes?** Hockey-stick revenue, missing costs, no buffer, poor cash timing, ignoring churn, and underestimating CAC.
- **How do metrics like MRR, churn, CAC, and LTV inform investors?** They show growth efficiency and sustainability.

## Summary

**Cash burn and runway formulas**

$$
\text{Net Burn} = \text{Cash Outflows} - \text{Cash Inflows}
$$

$$
\text{Runway (months)} = \frac{\text{Cash on Hand}}{\text{Net Burn per Month}}
$$

- Projections translate assumptions into a roadmap and fundraising plan.
- Scenario analysis exposes downside risk and informs buffer sizing.
- Investors focus on burn, runway, and unit economics to judge viability.
- Metrics like MRR, churn, CAC, and LTV describe growth quality and efficiency.
