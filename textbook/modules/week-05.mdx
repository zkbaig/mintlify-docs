## Key Concepts

- **Terminal value:** Forecast exit value used in VC method.
- **Discount rate:** Risk-adjusted rate used to discount future value (often 30–60% for VC).
- **Berkus Method:** Heuristic valuation assigning value to idea, prototype, team, etc.
- **Down round:** A funding round priced below the prior round, lowering the company’s paper value and signaling tougher conditions.
- **Anti-dilution:** Contract terms that adjust earlier investors’ ownership or conversion price if a later round is priced lower.
- **Pre-money vs. post-money:** Determines ownership percentages after investment.
- **Annual recurring revenue (ARR):** Annualized subscription revenue run rate.
- **Software-as-a-service (SaaS):** Subscription software delivered over the internet.
- **Year-over-year (YoY):** Growth compared with the same period in the prior year.
- **Free cash flow (FCF):** Cash generated after operating expenses and capital expenditures.
- **Price-to-earnings (P/E):** Valuation multiple of price relative to earnings.

## Main Content

### Chapter 1: Definitions and valuation lenses

Valuation is a **range** informed by method choice, assumptions, and the decision context in which the number will be used. Each method is a **comparative lens**: it compares your startup to a reference point (cash flows, peers, or exit outcomes) and then translates that comparison into a defensible range.

- **DCF:** Useful when you can credibly model cash flows; it breaks when margins and timing are guesswork, because small assumption changes swing the output.
- **Comparables:** Useful when your peers share similar growth and margin profiles; it breaks when the peer set is stale, hype-driven, or structurally different.
- **VC method:** Useful when the exit path and return target are clear; it breaks when exit timing or required ownership is unrealistic for the round size.

Taken together, the methods are less about precision and more about **consistency**: they should tell a coherent story about why this company, at this stage, merits a particular pricing band.

<Callout type="info" title="Triangulate, don’t anchor">
No single method is “right”; use multiple methods to bracket a range.
</Callout>

When you present a valuation, use **ranges, not point estimates**. Give a low/base/high band, explain what must be true for each band, and tie the top end to concrete milestones (e.g., hitting \$3M ARR, a 120% net dollar retention target, or a signed enterprise pilot). A range keeps you honest and gives investors room to align on assumptions rather than argue over a single number.

### Chapter 2: Frameworks and decision tools

This section formalizes the method logic so you can compare approaches on a like-for-like basis before you interpret the results or apply them in negotiations.

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="DCF logic">
    - Forecast cash flows (years 1–5)
    - Apply terminal multiple
    - Discount at 30–60% for startup risk
    - Compare the implied present value to financing needs and dilution tolerance
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Comparables logic">
    - Pick comparable companies
    - Choose relevant multiple (ARR, users)
    - Adjust for growth and margin differences
    - Compare where you sit within the peer range (top quartile vs. median)
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="VC method logic">
    - Start with a plausible exit value and timing
    - Apply the target return multiple
    - Back-solve to a post-money value today
    - Compare the ownership required to the amount of capital you need
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

With the method logic defined, the next sections apply the comparisons to concrete numbers before interpreting the results.

### Chapter 3: Worked example with numbers

**Scenario:** \$1M ARR SaaS with 100% YoY growth.

This example highlights how each method frames the same facts against a different benchmark, which is why the decision context (fundraising stage, capital need, and risk appetite) matters as much as the arithmetic.

Use the comparison below to see how the same company can yield different values depending on assumptions and method choice.

| Method | Assumption | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Comps | 6× ARR | \$6M |
| VC method | \$60M exit ÷ 10× | \$6M post-money |
| DCF | \$1.5M FCF by Year 5, 40% discount | ~\$5M |

**Range:** \$5M–\$7M post-money before term adjustments.

If the outputs are far apart, revisit growth, margins, and exit assumptions until the range is defensible and aligned to the decision you need to make (e.g., whether to prioritize speed of closing vs. a higher price).

### Chapter 4: Investor vs. founder perspective

The same range can be interpreted differently depending on whose decision is being made. Founders emphasize upside and narrative momentum, while investors emphasize downside protection and portfolio construction. Seeing these lenses side by side keeps the discussion comparative rather than adversarial.

| Dimension | Founder lens | Investor lens | Implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Forecasts | Optimistic growth | Base case with risk | Sensitivity checks |
| Multiples | Best peer comps | Median or discounted comps | Range negotiation |
| Risk | Vision upside | Downside protection | Terms matter |
| Timing | Raise at peak | Price with cycle risk | Market windows |

### Chapter 5: Method mismatch — when DCF, comps, and VC diverge

Method mismatch happens when each approach is anchored to **different risk views**, so the comparison set changes across methods instead of converging:

- **DCF diverges** when long-term margin, discount rate, or terminal value assumptions swing (common when cash flows are still negative and the long-term path is speculative).
- **Comps diverge** when the peer set is noisy, outdated, or in a different growth/margin regime, which makes the comparison set misleading.
- **VC method diverges** when exit multiple and target return assumptions are mismatched to cycle or dilution risk, producing a back-solve that is inconsistent with current market reality.

**Red flags for divergence**
Before you reconcile, diagnose which assumptions are driving the spread.
- DCF implies profitability that is not yet visible in unit economics.
- Comps rely on public multiples during a market bubble (or trough).
- VC method assumes aggressive exits without accounting for follow-on dilution.

### Chapter 6: Worked example — three different valuations and reconciliation

**Scenario:** \$2M ARR SaaS, 80% YoY growth, 70% gross margin, targeting a Series A.

| Method | Key assumption | Implied value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DCF | \$4M FCF in year 5, 45% discount, 10× terminal multiple | **\$12M** |
| Comps | Peer median 9× ARR, apply 1× turn discount for smaller scale | **\$16M** |
| VC method | \$160M exit in 5 years, 12× target return | **\$13M post-money** |

**Reconciling the gap**
The reconciliation should be explicit and comparable across methods so the decision-makers can see which assumptions are doing the work.
1. **Normalize assumptions:** Adjust DCF margins to match what the comps imply (e.g., 15–20% FCF margin) so both methods reference a consistent profitability profile.
2. **Check cycle effects:** If public SaaS comps fell from 12× to 9×, apply the lower multiple to both comps and terminal value to keep the comparison set consistent.
3. **Translate to a range:** Use the **overlap** (\$12M–\$16M) and defend a midpoint tied to milestones (e.g., \$14M with a plan to justify \$18M next round).

<Callout type="info" title="Reconciliation rule of thumb">
If methods are far apart, the **tightest overlap range** is the starting point; the narrative explains why you lean toward the top or bottom of the range.
</Callout>

### Chapter 7: Macro effects and shifting valuation ranges

Macro conditions shift **discount rates, exit multiples, and comps**, moving the entire valuation range.

- **Rate increases:** Higher discount rates compress DCF values and lower VC back-solves.
- **Comps compression:** Public multiple contraction (e.g., 12× to 7× ARR) cascades into private pricing.
- **Risk-off capital:** Investors raise return targets (10× → 15×), lowering VC-method valuations.

**Practical takeaway:** In tightening cycles, update **all three methods**, then widen the range to reflect heightened uncertainty.

### Chapter 8: How to defend a valuation in a term sheet discussion

Use this checklist to keep the valuation grounded and credible.

- [ ] **Anchor to triangulation:** Show DCF + comps + VC method and cite why the range is reasonable.
- [ ] **Explain method choice:** State which method is most reliable at the current stage and why.
- [ ] **Show sensitivity:** Highlight how value changes with growth, margins, or multiples.
- [ ] **Connect to milestones:** Tie the top end of the range to specific, achievable milestones.
- [ ] **Address dilution:** Acknowledge follow-on rounds and the effect on investor ownership.
- [ ] **Clarify terms impact:** Explain how liquidation preferences or option pools affect real value.
- [ ] **Use market data:** Bring 2–3 current comps and explain adjustments.
- [ ] **Offer a concession lever:** Keep non-price terms (board, liquidation, pro rata) in view.

**Founder guidance on ranges:** Walk in with a low/base/high range, not a single number. Lead with the base case, defend the high case with milestones, and explain the low case as the “deal-clearing” floor if the market tightens. Investors will push for a single price; your job is to keep the conversation on assumptions and the milestones that earn the top end.

## Worked Examples

### In-class activity: “Valuing a Venture – Art or Science?”

- **Scenario:** TechNova Inc. (SaaS) with \$2M current revenue, 100% YoY growth, \$20M projected revenue in 5 years.
- **Data:** Industry P/E ~12×, recent acquisitions ~5× revenue.
- **Calculations:**
  - Comparables: 5× current revenue vs. 5× projected revenue.
  - DCF: use profit margins and 40% discount rate with 12× terminal multiple.
  - VC method: 5× \$20M exit = \$100M; 10× target return → \$10M post-money.
- **Discussion:** Adjust valuations for team strength and competition; compare ranges (\$10M vs. \$50M+). Emphasize how each method frames “reasonable” in a different way.
- **Outcome:** Students see valuation variance and the role of assumptions, reinforcing why triangulation is the defensible starting point.

### Case study: HealthRx valuation dilemma

- **Background:** Digital health startup with \$200k ARR and early pilots; not yet profitable.
- **Term Sheet A:** \$10M post-money for \$2M investment (20% equity); justified by comparables.
- **Term Sheet B:** \$15M post-money for \$2M (13.3% equity) but with milestone-triggered anti-dilution.
- **Founder dilemma:** Lower valuation with clean terms vs. higher valuation with punitive conditions.
- **Context:** Founders expected ~\$20M based on hype; must evaluate realistic valuation and partner fit. The case is designed to separate headline price from economic reality.

### Mini-exercise: Choosing between two valuation ranges

**Scenario:** You receive two term sheets for the same company.

- **Range A:** \$8M–\$10M post-money with simple 1× non-participating liquidation preference.
- **Range B:** \$12M–\$14M post-money with a 1.5× participating preference and a broad-based anti-dilution clause.

**Your task:** Pick the range you would accept and justify it using the checklist below, explicitly stating how the terms alter the effective valuation.

**Decision rationale checklist**
- [ ] Does the higher range come with terms that reduce real ownership or exit proceeds?
- [ ] Is the valuation range supported by the triangulated methods (DCF, comps, VC)?
- [ ] How likely is a down round if the range is at the high end?
- [ ] Do the investor terms constrain future fundraising or hiring?
- [ ] Which range better aligns with credible milestones over the next 12–18 months?

### Discussion prompts and teaching notes

- **Which HealthRx term sheet is more justified and why?**
  - Teaching note: Use ARR multiples, risk, and partner value to justify A vs. B.
- **How do milestone-based anti-dilution terms change real valuation?**
  - Teaching note: They can reprice the deal lower if targets are missed.
- **What qualitative factors justify a higher valuation?**
  - Teaching note: Patents, strong team, strategic interest, large market.
- **What risks come with overvaluation?**
  - Teaching note: Down rounds, underwater options, and reputational damage.
- **What deal would you recommend?**
  - Teaching note: Consider clean terms vs. inflated valuation and investor fit.

## Pitfalls

<Callout type="warning" title="Pitfalls to Avoid">
The following issues often surface when teams prioritize a single method or ignore how terms change economic outcomes.
- Treating valuation as a single “correct” number rather than a range (e.g., insisting on \$20M post without showing what changes if growth slows).
- Ignoring option pool dilution when negotiating pre-money (e.g., a “\$12M pre” that becomes \$10M after a 15% refresh).
- Accepting high valuations with punitive milestones or anti-dilution terms (e.g., a headline \$25M that triggers repricing if ARR targets slip by one quarter).
- Using hockey-stick projections without credibility (e.g., assuming 3× pipeline conversion with no historical proof).
- Underestimating the impact of market cycles on pricing (e.g., a hype-era 15× ARR that resets to 7× and forces a down round).
- Letting hype drive the last round’s price without a fundamentals bridge (e.g., a “hot” Series A becomes a down round because churn rose and net retention fell).
</Callout>

## Readings

### Required Readings

- **Sahlman & Scherlis (2009). VC Method note.** Forecast exit, apply high discount rate, and choose terminal multiples.
- **Darden note (2016). Valuing the Early-Stage Company.** Comparables, net present value (NPV), decision trees, and qualitative judgment.
- **Payne (2007). VC Method for pre-revenue.** Step-by-step terminal value and return on investment (ROI) calculations.

### Optional Readings

- **Brex Journal (2021).** Eight valuation methods; Berkus Method details (up to \$500k per factor, ~\$2M cap).
- **Equidam (2023).** Methods to use/avoid; warns against earnings/book-value methods for startups.

## Summary

- Valuation methods triangulate across DCF, comparables, and VC method assumptions.
- High discount rates and return multiples reflect VC risk and dilution.
- Term-sheet terms can make a “high valuation” economically worse.
