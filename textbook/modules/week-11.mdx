## Key concepts + definitions

- **Liability of foreignness:** Added friction and information asymmetry in cross-border deals.
- **Cross-border syndicates:** Co-investments with local partners to reduce risk.
- **Regulatory constraints:** Foreign ownership, data rules, and listing restrictions.
- **Jurisdiction flips:** Delaware/Cayman holding structures for investor friendliness.

## Main content

### Topic narrative

- VC is global, with distinct regional ecosystems and regulatory environments.
- U.S. remains dominant (70–75% of global VC value in early 2025) driven by mega-deals.
- Europe is growing but fragmented; hubs include London, Berlin, Paris, Nordics.
- Asia is diverse: China and India are major markets but face regulatory and geopolitical shifts.
- Emerging regions (LATAM, Africa, SE Asia) show growth but have currency and exit constraints.
- Cross-border investing offers valuation arbitrage and diversification but adds due diligence costs.
- Risks include distance, culture, legal enforceability, currency, and exit limitations.
- Mitigations: partner with local VCs, focus on later stages, use neutral jurisdictions.
- Global competition and mega-funds can inflate valuations in emerging markets.
- Policy and geopolitics (CFIUS, data rules, antitrust) shape deal feasibility.

## Worked examples

### In-class activity: Cross-border pitch meeting

- **Roles:** Founder from an emerging market pitches to North American VCs.
- **Focus:** Exit feasibility, legal structure, currency risk, and local partners.
- **Outcome:** Identify red flags and pitch adaptations for global investors.

### Case study: SoftWear VR cross-border Series B

- **Company:** Toronto-based VR startup expanding to Europe and Asia.
- **Investors:** U.S. lead, European VC, Japanese VC with valuation concerns.
- **Issues:** Delaware flip, due diligence distance, valuation gap, exit preferences.
- **Board challenges:** Time zones, strategy alignment, and governance cohesion.

## Pitfalls / common mistakes

- Assuming U.S. term sheets translate seamlessly across jurisdictions.
- Ignoring currency and exit market constraints in emerging regions.
- Overlooking cultural communication gaps in cross-border governance.
- Failing to structure legal entities for future exit flexibility.

- **How do VC markets vary internationally?** U.S. dominates; Europe/Asia differ by fragmentation and regulation.
- **What are cross-border risks?** Distance, legal differences, currency, and exit uncertainty.
- **How do investors mitigate foreignness?** Local partners, later-stage focus, jurisdiction flips.
- **How do global trends affect VC?** Geopolitics and regulatory shifts can reshape capital flows.
- **How do exits work globally?** Many rely on foreign IPOs or strategic M&A.

## Summary + key formulas

- Global VC expands opportunity but adds legal, cultural, and macro risks.
- Cross-border success depends on governance, structure, and partner alignment.
- Exit planning is central when local markets are thin.

### Discussion questions and teaching notes

- **Should SoftWear VR flip to Delaware for Series B?**
  - Teaching note: Balance investor familiarity vs. local incentives and tax.
- **How can BrightEast be convinced on valuation?**
  - Teaching note: Use global comps and structured milestones.
- **What governance practices support a global board?**
  - Teaching note: Clear materials, rotating times, and independent facilitation.
- **How should the company align exit expectations?**
  - Teaching note: Discuss dual-track IPO/M&A early and revisit with market shifts.
