## Topic Narrative
Venture capital, once heavily concentrated in Silicon Valley and a few other U.S. hubs, has become an increasingly global industry . Today, significant startup funding and innovation clusters span the globe – from North America to Europe, Asia, Latin America, and Africa – each with its own ecosystem characteristics. For a venture investor, understanding global markets means recognizing how local economic conditions, culture, and regulations shape startup trajectories and investment opportunities. It also means dealing with the complexities of cross-border investing , where capital, entrepreneurs, and ideas flow across countries. This week examines the differences in major VC markets, the rise of venture activity outside the U.S., and the challenges and rewards of investing internationally. North America (especially the U.S.) remains the largest VC market by far, but its share of global venture funding has evolved. In early 2025, the U.S. accounted for roughly 70–75% of global VC investment by value , partly due to massive late-stage deals in areas like AI. The U.S. offers deep capital pools, a culture of entrepreneurship, large domestic markets, and well-trodden exit pathways (Nasdaq/NYSE for IPOs, frequent tech M&A). Silicon Valley, New York, Boston and emerging hubs like Austin or Toronto (in Canada) have robust startup networks. The presence of institutional investors, experienced founders, and corporate acquirers in North America creates a rich VC environment. By contrast, Europe has historically lagged in VC volume but has grown steadily. The European VC market is more fragmented by country, with major hubs in the UK (London), Germany (Berlin, Munich), France (Paris), Nordics, etc. European startups often face a smaller unified market (despite the EU) and historically relied more on bank financing, but venture funding has expanded. In Q1 2025, Europe saw about $18 billion across ~1,883 deals – resilient relative to its past, though still a fraction of U.S. activity. Notably, Europe has produced successful startups (Spotify, Adyen, Klarna, etc.) and increasingly retains talent rather than losing it to the U.S., thanks in part to local funding growth and supportive policies (EU programs, national innovation grants). 42 45 46 47 12 Asia is a diverse landscape. China surged in the 2010s to become the second-largest VC market, with big tech ecosystems in Beijing, Shanghai, and Shenzhen, and strong government support. However, recent geopolitical tensions and regulatory crackdowns on tech (e.g., restrictions on data and foreign listings) have cooled foreign VC involvement in China and somewhat dampened domestic startup valuations. In early 2025, VC investment in Asia-Pacific fell to a record low (~$12.9B in Q1’25) , reflecting cautious sentiment and lower mega-deals. Still, China and India each have robust startup scenes. India has risen as a major market with Bangalore’s tech scene and significant global investor interest, although many Indian startups expand to global markets or flip jurisdictions (some incorporate in Singapore or the U.S. for investor friendliness). Southeast Asia , Latin America , and Africa – while smaller in absolute terms – have shown some of the fastest growth rates in venture activity until the recent pullback. These regions often attract global VCs seeking growth (for example, Brazil’s fintech boom saw big U.S. VC investments; Nigeria and Kenya have become African fintech hubs). Each region poses unique factors: Latin America’s volatile currencies and smaller exit markets, Africa’s infrastructure gaps but huge addressable problems and leapfrog tech adoption, Southeast Asia’s heterogeneous markets and increasing government startup programs. Investing across borders introduces both opportunities and challenges . On the opportunity side, VCs can arbitrage valuation differences (startups in emerging markets might have lower valuations relative to growth potential), tap into large untapped markets (e.g., India’s 1.4 billion population with rising internet use), and diversify their portfolios geographically. Some VCs pursue a “global specialist” strategy – focusing on a sector (say fintech or climate tech) but investing worldwide to find the best teams . Cross-border syndicates have also become common: it’s not unusual for a Silicon Valley firm to co-invest with a local Asian or European VC in a round. Furthermore, many startups are “born global” – scaling users internationally (think of SaaS companies selling globally online, or consumer apps spreading across countries), so having international investors can provide networks for market entry and follow-on funding abroad. However, there are significant “liabilities of foreignness” when investing internationally . VCs venturing outside their home turf encounter geographical distance , which complicates hands-on mentoring and due diligence – it’s harder to attend board meetings or recruit talent for a startup an ocean away. Cultural differences can affect everything from how entrepreneurs pitch (and whether the VC understands the nuance) to how business is conducted (relationship-driven networks, negotiation styles, etc.). Institutional and legal differences are paramount: each country has its regulations on business formation, intellectual property, labor, and critically, on foreign investment (for instance, some countries have restrictions on foreign ownership in certain sectors). VCs must navigate differing legal frameworks – for example, terms like liquidation preferences or vesting might be interpreted differently under another country’s corporate law. These differences can increase transaction costs and risks. Academic research confirms that cross- border VC deals often have higher information asymmetries and agency costs , leading investors to require higher potential returns to compensate . Indeed, one study found cross-border VC investments tend to have lower IPO likelihood and slightly lower returns on average , perhaps due to those added frictions . Successful cross-border investors often mitigate these risks by partnering with local VC firms (who know the market and can monitor the startup closely), and by focusing on later stages where the company is more proven, or on sectors where the investor has deep expertise that travels across borders. Another dimension is currency and macroeconomic risk . Venture investments are illiquid and long-term, so currency fluctuations can impact foreign LPs’ returns – e.g., a U.S. VC investing in Brazilian reais could see gains wiped out by currency depreciation. Political and economic instability can also affect exits – a promising startup might have trouble exiting locally if capital markets are underdeveloped (many foreign 48 49 50 51 51 52 13 startups seek to IPO in the U.S. or be acquired by multinational firms for this reason). High-profile cases include Chinese tech companies listing on NYSE or Nasdaq because domestic exchanges or Hong Kong couldn’t match the valuation or liquidity (though this is changing with new boards in Shanghai/Shenzhen and tighter overseas listing rules). As a result, cross-border VCs often insist on legal structures like the startup incorporating in a neutral jurisdiction (e.g., a Cayman holding company commonly used for Chinese or Indian startups seeking foreign VC, or Delaware corp for others) to facilitate future exits and ensure contract enforceability. The global nature of venture also means competition is global . Top U.S. VC firms have expanded footprints – opening offices in London, Singapore, or Bengaluru – and large pools of capital (like SoftBank’s Vision Fund or Tiger Global) have roamed worldwide, driving up valuations in emerging markets during peak periods. This has benefits (more capital for startups) but also challenges local investors and can create overheated situations. We saw examples where an influx of foreign capital in a nascent ecosystem (like quick rise of unicorns in Southeast Asia or Latin America around 2020–21) led to valuations outpacing local exit options. Cross-border investing thus requires a nuanced understanding of exit pathways in that region: Will there be local IPO opportunities (e.g., India created a surge of tech IPOs like Zomato, Paytm in 2021, though some underperformed)? Are global acquirers interested in that market’s startups (e.g., Uber acquiring Careem in Middle East)? Finally, policy and geopolitics significantly influence global VC. Governments worldwide vie to nurture startup ecosystems (through tax incentives, startup visas, public venture funds, etc.). For instance, countries like Israel famously leveraged military R&D and government programs (like the Yozma fund) to catalyze a VC hub that is highly successful and globally connected. On the flip side, rising protectionism and national security concerns have put scrutiny on cross-border tech investments – the U.S. has tightened CFIUS reviews on foreign (especially Chinese) investment in sensitive tech, and China has restricted data-rich companies from taking foreign money or listing abroad without approval. These trends can bifurcate the global VC landscape (some call it “tech decoupling”). Venture investors must be aware of and adaptable to these currents – for example, focusing on regions or sectors where cross-border collaboration is welcomed, and structuring deals to comply with all jurisdictions involved. In summary, global venture capital offers a broader canvas for finding innovation and returns, but success requires embracing local context, managing additional layers of risk, and often working with partners. The North American lens is still vital – many global startups seek expertise or capital from U.S. VCs – but a well- informed VC today keeps a global awareness , recognizing where the next breakout companies can emerge and how to support them across borders.
## Key Questions & Takeaways
How do VC market sizes and styles vary internationally? VC activity is heavily skewed to the U.S., which typically attracts the largest share of funding (over half of global VC dollars) due to its huge investor base and exit markets . However, other regions are growing their VC ecosystems – e.g., Europe now regularly produces “unicorns” and has more local fund managers than a decade ago, and Asia (led by China and India) saw a venture boom with some slowdown recently. The style of investing can differ: U.S. deals often have bigger round sizes and higher valuations, while in emerging markets VCs might start with smaller rounds tailored to local purchasing power. Additionally, sectors can differ by region (for instance, fintech and mobile solutions have been big in
- 45 14 Africa where traditional infrastructure is lacking, whereas Europe has strength in deep tech like fintech, healthtech, etc., often leveraging strong research institutions). What are the challenges of cross-border VC deals? Distance and information : It’s harder to perform due diligence and support a startup from afar – time zones, lack of on-the-ground networks, and unfamiliarity with local consumer behavior can impede a foreign VC’s effectiveness. Legal and regulatory : Foreign investors face varied legal systems; contract enforcement or shareholder rights may be less predictable. For example, a term sheet standard in Silicon Valley might not be enforceable as-is under another country’s laws. Many cross-border deals thus involve legal structures like offshore entities or use standardized documents (NVCA templates) to minimize confusion . Foreign exchange and exit risk : A VC may back an overseas startup that grows well, but if the local stock market is not receptive to tech IPOs or if foreign M&A is restricted, the exit options could be limited. Moreover, currency devaluation can eat into returns when converting back to the VC’s base currency. These challenges mean cross-border VCs often partner with local co- investors, stick to sectors they know well, and sometimes require a higher ownership stake or lower valuation to compensate for added risk. Indeed, research indicates cross-border investments often demand a premium: VCs might seek higher projected IRR knowing some friction will reduce actual outcomes . How do VCs mitigate cross-border risks? Successful global investors employ several strategies: Local partnerships – aligning with a reputable local VC or hiring local team members provides cultural and market insight, helping navigate nuances and source better deals. Stage focus – some invest cross-border only at later stages, once the company has traction and clearer metrics, to reduce early uncertainty (for instance, an American growth fund might join a Series C in Brazil, leaving earlier rounds to local seed funds). Governance measures – foreign VCs may insist on certain protections: e.g., the company incorporating in a neutral jurisdiction (many Indian startups are Delaware or Singapore entities) so that U.S.-style corporate governance applies, or securing board seats and information rights to stay informed. Currency hedging is rare due to long time horizons, but VCs may concentrate on markets with relatively stable macro environments or those pegged to their currency. Additionally, many cross-border VCs focus on exportable models – startups that can expand beyond their home country, providing more exit avenues (like a Southeast Asian startup expanding regionally or aiming for U.S. customers, etc.). How do global trends affect venture investing? Global economic and political trends can open or constrain opportunities. For example, the massive digital adoption in emerging markets (smartphone penetration, e-commerce growth) has enabled entire new markets for startups – a trend that savvy VCs leveraged in the 2010s to back “the Uber of X country” or “the Stripe of Y region.” On the other hand, geopolitical tensions (such as U.S.–China trade disputes) have led to reduced cross-investment; U.S. VC funding of Chinese startups has dropped sharply and vice versa. Regulations like Europe’s GDPR (data privacy law) can affect how certain products scale globally (a U.S. health startup must comply with EU rules if expanding). Another trend is government venture initiatives : many governments now co-invest or provide matching funds to stimulate local VC (e.g., Singapore’s Temasek and EDBI actively invest, the EU’s investment fund supports venture). These can be a boon to VCs operating in those regions (extra capital, risk-sharing) but also sometimes come with strings (mandates to invest in certain industries or locations). In summary, VCs must remain globally aware: a recession in one region, a boom in another, new trade policies or tax incentives – all can shift where the attractive investments are and how to approach them. What is the role of cross-border VC in startup globalization? Cross-border VCs don’t just bring money; they often bring networks, expertise, and validation. For instance, when a well-known Silicon Valley fund invests in an emerging-market startup, it can help that company attract customers or additional talent by signaling global credibility. Some U.S. VCs have actively facilitated bringing foreign startups to the U.S. market (sometimes even reincorporating them in the U.S. and relocating the founders). This raises an interesting effect: brain drain vs. brain circulation . In some cases, foreign VC investment has led startups to shift HQ to the U.S. or another hub (to be closer to markets or investors), potentially “relocating” innovation globally . However, increasingly, global talent flow is multi- directional – entrepreneurs might study or work abroad and then return home to start companies, and VCs follow them. Cross-border investing thus plays a key role in the global diffusion of innovation practices and capital. An awareness of this allows venture investors to spot trends (e.g., a successful model in one country being adapted to another) and understand that competition is global – if you’re not looking internationally, another foreign fund might swoop into a promising deal in your backyard (and vice versa). How do exits work for global startups? A critical consideration: ultimately, LPs expect returns via exits, and for cross-border deals one must plan how those exits can happen. Many startups in smaller markets aim for international exits – for example, a Southeast Asian startup might aim to be bought by a U.S. tech giant, or a Latin American fintech might list on the NASDAQ if local markets are too small. VCs often help pave these paths by connecting founders with international investment banks or acquirers. There are also regional consolidations – e.g., a bigger European startup acquiring smaller ones across EU to scale before a sale or IPO, or a regional champion emerging that can go public in London or New York. Knowing the realistic exit routes in a given country (Can they IPO domestically? Will regulators allow an acquisition by a foreign company? Are secondaries common for interim liquidity?) is crucial when investing there. For instance, in China a prevalent exit for venture-backed companies was U.S. IPOs or Hong Kong listings – when those became harder due to regulations, valuations and VC appetite adjusted. In some frontier markets, trade sales to global companies or private equity are the primary route since public markets are undeveloped. Venture investors must set expectations accordingly and often work creatively (sometimes holding companies longer or structuring earn-outs in acquisitions) to successfully exit.

## Optional Readings
Schertler, A. & Tykvová, T. (2020). “Cross-Border Venture Capital Investments: The Impact of Foreignness on Returns.” An academic study analyzing thousands of VC deals worldwide. It finds that cross-border VC deals tend to exit faster but at lower returns and with fewer IPOs than domestic deals . The authors attribute this to the “liability of foreignness” – distance, cultural and institutional gaps increase costs and information asymmetry, which can reduce ultimate performance . However, they also note cross-border VC grew to ~40% of deals pre-2007 and is pursued for diversification and accessing opportunities despite lower average returns . For those interested in a deeper quantitative perspective, this paper provides evidence and reasons behind the challenges of international VC investing. SEC (2024). “Analysis of Regulation Crowdfunding Under the JOBS Act” (Section on Global Comparison). While focused on U.S. equity crowdfunding data, this SEC report’s introduction provides context on how alternate financing (like crowdfunding) remains relatively small compared to venture capital globally, and it indirectly highlights the differences in funding environments across countries . For example, it notes that in 2024 U.S. Regulation CF offerings raised only ~$340M – underscoring that the U.S. relies far more on traditional VC than crowd finance, whereas some other countries have more government or bank involvement. This optional piece is useful to understand how global startups might seek funding outside VC (grants, crowds, etc.) and that the prevalence of VC itself varies – some ecosystems are VC-heavy, others find capital through alternate means.
## In-Class Activity
Cross-Border Investment Pitch Instructions: Students will engage in a mock pitch meeting for a cross-border investment. One student (or team) acts as the founder of a startup from a non-North American country (e.g., a healthtech startup from Brazil or a fintech from Kenya), and the other side are VCs from North America considering investing. The founder must pitch the company, and importantly, address typical concerns a foreign VC would have: How will you expand beyond your local market? What’s the regulatory environment? How do we get comfortable with the team from afar? The VC side will ask questions focusing on cross-border issues (e.g., “What’s your exit plan – local IPO or U.S. listing?” “How do you handle currency risk?” “Who are your local investors and mentors?”). Objectives: This activity gets students thinking practically about how an entrepreneur from an emerging market can attract global capital, and conversely what a VC would diligence. It trains students to consider local context explanations (founder explaining market size and growth, perhaps drawing parallels like “We are the Shopify of Southeast Asia” to help foreign investors relate) and to consider what adaptations VCs might require (for instance, maybe the VC requests the startup reincorporate in Delaware or agrees only if a reputable local co-investor joins the round). By simulating this interaction, students learn to pinpoint key cross-border evaluation criteria: market scalability, legal structure, exit feasibility, and trust in the team’s ability to bridge cultures. Materials: A profile of the fictional startup is given to the “founder” student, including country, product, current traction, and funding needs. The “VC” students get a brief on that country’s venture scene (e.g., average VC round sizes there, any known recent exits, economic conditions). Both have a checklist of considerations (founder’s: how to reassure on exit and legal; VC’s: risk factors like currency, local competition). They then conduct a 10-minute pitch Q&A.

## Case Study
SoftWear VR: A Cross-Border Growth Story Background: SoftWear VR is a startup based in Toronto, Canada , that developed an innovative virtual reality software for fashion design collaboration. It enables clothing designers and manufacturers (who might be in different countries) to meet in a virtual workspace, manipulating 3D garment models. The company gained traction in North America and Europe, and is now expanding into Asia. SoftWear VR raised a Series A from Canadian and U.S. VCs. Now, they are seeking a larger Series B (~$30M) to fuel global growth. Several investors from different regions are interested: Alpha VC from Silicon Valley (who led the A round), EuroCapital from London, and a potential new lead, BrightEast Ventures from Japan. The case follows SoftWear VR’s journey as a cross-border venture and the considerations each investor brings. Global Expansion: The startup’s product is inherently global (fashion industry supply chains are international). They already serve clients in the U.S., UK, and are piloting with a manufacturer in Vietnam. They have a small sales office in Paris and plan to open one in Tokyo. This raises questions of how to manage a distributed team and clients – e.g., time zones, customer support in multiple languages. It also means navigating different regulatory environments , such as data security/privacy laws in the EU (GDPR) affecting how VR collaboration data is stored. Cross-Border Investment Issues: - Deal Structure: For the Series B, SoftWear VR (a Canadian corporation) may set up a U.S. Delaware parent entity to make it easier for U.S. and other foreign VCs to invest (a flip or an equivalent structure), as advised by Alpha VC. EuroCapital is fine with this, but BrightEast (Japan) has questions about tax implications of investing in a Delaware vs. Canadian entity. - Due Diligence and Trust: BrightEast Ventures has not worked with this team before and is unfamiliar with the Toronto tech scene. They worry about not being geographically close. To mitigate this, they consider co- investing alongside Alpha VC, who has a board seat and deep knowledge of the company. BrightEast’s partners also plan extra due diligence: they engage a third-party tech consultant in Canada to evaluate SoftWear’s tech, and set up calls with some of the startup’s European clients to verify the product’s global usefulness. - Valuation and Market Understanding: There’s debate on valuation. Alpha VC and EuroCapital, more familiar with VR and enterprise software markets, value the company at ~$150M pre-money based on growth. BrightEast is more conservative, referencing that in Japan, enterprise VR startups exit at lower 18 multiples; they feel $150M is high and would be more comfortable at $120M. This difference stems from varying perspectives on market potential and perhaps home bias. The company must articulate why the higher valuation is justified given a global market size and not just local comps. - Exit Outlook: Each investor is considering exits: Alpha VC is U.S.-focused and imagines a potential NASDAQ IPO if SoftWear VR becomes a category leader, or acquisition by a big U.S. tech firm (since many are developing VR/AR platforms). EuroCapital wonders if an EU exit is possible (maybe a large software conglomerate in Europe might buy SoftWear, or perhaps an IPO on a European exchange, although historically those have lower valuations for tech). BrightEast ponders if there’s an Asian angle – for instance, a partnership or eventual sale to a Japanese electronics giant. They also bring up currency: the startup earns a lot in USD and Euros, but if they expand in Asia, currency management might be needed. Case Questions: 1. How should SoftWear VR structure its Series B to accommodate international investors? Discuss the pros/cons of the company being a Canadian entity vs. flipping to a U.S. Delaware corporation for this round. Consider legal simplicity, familiarity for foreign VCs, tax implications, and any Canadian government incentives they might lose or keep. What would you advise and why? 2. What concerns might BrightEast Ventures (Japan) have, and how can the company and other VCs address them? List at least three cross-border due diligence or risk points BrightEast is evaluating (e.g., corporate governance differences, reporting transparency, ability to guide a foreign startup). How can SoftWear VR build trust with a new investor from another continent (for example, inviting them to take an observer board seat, providing extra rights like information rights or vetoes to comfort them, or simply leveraging Alpha VC’s involvement as a credibility signal)? 3. Valuation discrepancy: How should the lead investors reconcile the different valuation viewpoints? If you were the CEO of SoftWear VR, how would you justify the higher valuation to BrightEast using global market logic (perhaps citing the total addressable market of global fashion collaboration, the startup’s unique global client base, and comparisons to U.S. VR companies’ valuations)? Conversely, if a compromise is needed, what tools could you use – for instance, could you structure part of the round as tranched investment or with earn-out milestones that reassure the cautious investor? 4. Managing a global board and operations: With a potential board comprising a Silicon Valley VC, a European VC, and possibly a Japanese observer, what challenges and benefits arise? Consider meeting scheduling (time zones), strategic input (diverse perspectives could help the company enter Europe and Asia), and possible misalignment (one investor might push for strategies favoring their region). How can the CEO ensure the board remains cohesive and all investors are on the same page regarding company strategy and exit plans? What governance practices (like having board materials in advance, rotating meeting times, hiring a seasoned CFO to handle complex reporting) might help? 5. Exit strategy alignment: Each investor has their own exit preference (U.S. IPO vs. European M&A vs. other). How can the company and investors align on a general exit strategy? Should they explicitly discuss likely exit routes at the time of investment (for example, agree that in 3-4 years they’ll seek a dual-track process of IPO or sale)? How do macro factors (like if U.S.–China relations tighten, maybe a Chinese buyer is off the table, or if European markets stay weaker, a U.S. IPO yields better returns) influence these plans? Discuss the importance of being flexible yet having a shared vision (perhaps aiming for the “best possible exit” wherever it may come, and each investor helping in their region to scout opportunities). Discussion: This case highlights the practical complexities of a cross-border VC deal . Students will likely conclude that SoftWear VR should indeed flip to a Delaware C-corp to smooth the investment (a common practice for Canadian startups scaling globally). They will discuss how the company can leverage each investor’s strengths: e.g., use BrightEast’s connections to enter Japan, EuroCapital’s to navigate EU 19 enterprise clients, and Alpha VC’s for U.S. tech partnerships – turning the diverse investor base into an asset rather than a hurdle. On valuation, they might propose a middle ground or acceptance that global markets command higher multiples if growth is global (educating BrightEast on those facts, possibly using data that half of 2024’s global VC funding was into AI companies, showing big appetite which could analogously apply to VR ). The discussion on the board will bring out the importance of cultural sensitivity and communication – perhaps one outcome is the CEO deciding to hold two board calls (one at a time friendly to U.S./Europe, another for Asia) or to do annual in-person board meetings rotating locations. Finally, in exit alignment, students should see that while each investor might have a preferred path, they all want the company to succeed and thus must coordinate to pursue the optimal exit opportunity when time comes. The case ends positively, imagining SoftWear VR raising the Series B successfully and using its internationally savvy board to become a truly global tech success – precisely the kind of outcome cross- border venture capital strives to achieve.
